One Last Thing

I started this book by asking you to pick up the phone closest to you, feel its weight, and notice how strange it is that an object of such complexity sits in your pocket unremarked.

Do that one more time now.

Look at it. Since the beginning of this book you’ve learned what’s inside it — the sand it came from, the fabs that made it, the cathedrals where its counterparts run, the energy and water and materials it took, the attention it was built to extract from you, the people who live next to the infrastructure that serves it, the financial structure that paid for the buildout, the small number of people whose decisions shape what it can do and what it does to others. You know more about this object than its designers ever expected its users to care to know.

That knowledge has value. Not because it lets you personally fix the world — it doesn’t. Because the world is, increasingly, built of things like this: invisible, concentrated, consequential. And the people who shape that world well, from here out, will be the ones who understand what they’re looking at.

You understand what you’re looking at now. More than most adults. That’s an asset. Get good at something real, and use it.

The book is done.

You are not.

Go.

A note on sources: this chapter reflects the author’s judgment more than the others, and you should read it that way. The personal-action leverage estimates are rough — drawn from life-cycle assessments and energy modeling where those exist, and from reasoning where they don’t. The claim that “keep your devices longer” is the highest-leverage personal action is, I believe, well-supported by the literature on device-lifecycle impact; manufacturers’ published assessments are a good place to check. The claims about civic action and careers are less quantifiable, and reasonable people disagree about them.

What I’m most confident of is the smallest and least quantifiable claim in the chapter: that real competence, the kind you can make an honest living from, is what gives a person both leverage and the freedom to keep their integrity. I’ve watched the alternative enough times to trust it.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The author says you have read the usual list before and already suspect most of it does almost nothing. Was he right about you?
  2. Shelf One is pure symbol, and he names it clearly because spending your attention on things that do not work is the worst outcome. Which symbolic action were you most attached to?
  3. His least quantifiable claim is the one he is most confident of: that real competence gives a person both leverage and the freedom to keep their integrity. Do you believe it? What would test it?

Shelf Two: Things That Matter a Little

These are real. They don’t change the world; they change one person’s footprint by a measurable but modest amount. In aggregate, if a lot of people did them, they’d matter more.

Keep your devices longer. This is the one personal-consumption choice in the whole chapter worth real emphasis. If everyone used their phones for four years instead of two, the total impact of the phone industry would roughly halve — both upstream in mining and manufacturing and downstream in e-waste. Nothing else you do with your own device comes close. The chip is expensive to make; using it twice as long cuts that expense in half. It’s the boring answer, and it’s the right one.

A phone bought in 2026 will, for most people, handle everything you’d ask of it in 2030. Same for laptops. Batteries and screens can be replaced; most of what makes a device feel “old” is software nagging you to upgrade. The nag is designed to take your money. Resist it.

Recycle electronics properly when you do dispose. Find a certified recycler (R2 or e-Stewards in the US; the WEEE system in Europe). Don’t put electronics in the trash, and don’t let them sit in a drawer forever. The difference between proper recycling and improper disposal is real — it decides whether the materials are recovered in controlled facilities or processed in dangerous conditions somewhere else.

Eat less beef. I know this feels off-topic. It isn’t. The embodied cost of your daily diet is much larger than the embodied cost of your daily computing, and beef is disproportionately responsible. If you wanted to make one significant personal environmental choice, the calorie mix of your diet is roughly a hundred times more consequential than anything you do with your devices. For most rich-country kids, the footprint of lunch is larger than the footprint of the phone.

Use less air conditioning, when you can. AC is the largest single energy expense in most American homes and the fastest-growing. A few degrees on the thermostat in summer is worth more, in energy, than your entire computing footprint — genuinely worth doing where it doesn’t risk your health. (In Phoenix that safe range is small; in Boston it’s larger.)

Pay attention to what you buy. Not in the “consume ethically” sense, which is mostly impossible given how supply chains work, but in the plain sense: don’t buy what you don’t need, don’t throw away what works, don’t replace things because of marketing. The consumption you don’t do is the only kind with a clean conscience.

Useful, all of it. None of it world-changing. If the world needs to change, something else has to happen — and that’s the next shelf.

Shelf Four: Things That Are Huge, If You Want Them To Be

A small number of people, in each generation, end up doing outsized amounts of good. They’re not necessarily smarter or luckier than average. They’re usually people who decided — often in their teens or twenties — to take one specific problem seriously and stay with it.

You don’t have to be one of them. Most people aren’t, and that’s fine. But if you might be, it helps to know the shape so you can choose it.

The shape is: pick a problem that matters, go deep over years until you’re at the frontier of what’s actually known about it — not a casual understanding, a real one — and then work on moving the frontier. That’s how every significant change in any technical field has ever been made. It isn’t magic. It’s time, attention, and the selection of what to spend both on.

For the issues in this book, the problems that could use more serious people include making AI systems safer and more interpretable, making chips more energy-efficient, cleaning up electronic-waste supply chains, designing data centers that don’t harm local water, writing regulations for AI and for siting that actually hold, developing battery chemistries that don’t depend on cobalt mined under bad conditions, improving grid integration so more of the computing runs on clean power, and doing the serious financial and economic analysis of the buildout’s capital structure that almost no one with real numeracy is doing. There are real people working on each of these right now, and more are needed for all of them.

These aren’t vague. The path from “fourteen-year-old who’s interested in how chips are made” to “adult who works on chip efficiency” is a real path; people walk it every year. The tools are there, the questions are open, the mentors exist. The difference between “I wish someone would fix this” and “I’m someone who might” is mostly whether you put in the decade it takes to get good. A decade sounds long. You have several of them ahead of you.

Same caution as with law: being known for caring about a problem is not the same as being able to fix it. The version that matters is the quiet one — competence, not profile. The work is the point.

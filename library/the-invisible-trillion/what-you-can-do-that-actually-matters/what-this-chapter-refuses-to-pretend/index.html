The Invisible Trillion · What You Can Do That Actually Matters

What This Chapter Refuses to Pretend

I want to close by saying clearly what this chapter has not said, because it matters.

It has not said that you personally are responsible for solving the environmental or political problems of the tech industry. You aren’t. The companies making these decisions are making them; the governments regulating them are regulating them, or not; the infrastructure is being built; the costs are being imposed. Individual-responsibility rhetoric has, historically, been used to pull attention from structural causes to personal habits while the structural causes kept going. Don’t fall for it. Your job is not to be the last line of defense for a system that was never designed to be defended by individuals.

It has not said that everything is fine and tech will fix itself. The problems in this book are real, the solutions aren’t inevitable, and good outcomes need specific people to do specific things. Some of those people will be you, eventually. Most will be others. Not all of them will succeed. The future is genuinely open, and pretending it’s either doomed or saved is just an excuse not to engage with what’s actually happening.

It has not said that you have to care about any of this. You don’t. You get to care about whatever you care about. If it’s art, music, biology, friendship, God, mathematics — those are legitimate and worth a life. I think these issues are important, and that some of you reading this should give them serious attention. Some of you shouldn’t, and that’s fine too.

What it has said is this. If you’re going to engage, engage seriously. Don’t get captured by the performative version. Don’t confuse gesture with action. Notice who has leverage, and build the kind of self that can have leverage over decades — which mostly means getting genuinely good at one real thing. Protect your attention. Show up to the rooms where decisions are made. Evaluate claims piece by piece instead of swallowing narratives whole. Notice when you’re being sold something. Keep caring about people.

This is the closing chapter of a book about infrastructure, and it’s ending with advice that could go in almost any book. That’s not an accident. The infrastructure is large and specific; the response to it, for most readers, is to become the kind of person who handles large and specific things well. There’s no special move for AI and data centers. There’s only the ordinary, difficult business of being a thoughtful person in a world that rewards thoughtlessness, and doing it anyway, for a long time.

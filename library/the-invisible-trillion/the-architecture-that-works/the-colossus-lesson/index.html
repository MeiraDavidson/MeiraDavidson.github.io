The Colossus Lesson

Come back to Colossus 1, because it’s the cleanest example of the inflation working in real life.

It went up at absurd speed — nineteen days from concept to the first operational cluster, the full build trailing over the next eighteen months — at a cost somewhere around $40 billion, to train Grok. The cooling was sized for full training load. The power was sized for full training load. Memphis was picked for fast permitting and grid access. The gas turbines went in without permits because the company did the math and decided the legal exposure was cheaper than the wait. All of it rested on one assumption: that xAI’s own frontier training would fill the building, and that filling it was do-or-die for xAI’s standing as a frontier lab.

Eighteen months on, with the thing running, it sat at roughly 11% MFU. The training xAI was actually doing didn’t need the capacity — maybe Grok’s requirements came in smaller than projected, maybe the training was less efficient than hoped, maybe the shifting market simply made more Grok capability worth less. Whatever the cause, the facility had been built ahead of its own creator’s demand. Eighteen months after that, in May 2026, xAI leased the whole thing to Anthropic and Google for something like $26 billion a year against the $40 billion build — paying it off in about a year and a half.

On a spreadsheet, the rental is fine. But look at what it does not prove. It doesn’t prove there was demand for the capacity before xAI built it. It proves that once the capacity existed, renters turned up. Their demand — Anthropic’s appetite for Claude inference, Google’s for Gemini Enterprise capacity — was real, but it wasn’t the demand that justified pouring the foundation. xAI bet its own training would fill the place. The bet was wrong. The building stands anyway, and other labs fill it.

That’s the inflation in miniature: capacity built on a thesis about one kind of demand, the demand failing to arrive in that form, the capacity repurposed to a different and usually thinner use that’s enough to keep the lights on. The cost gets recovered. The story that was pitched doesn’t survive. And the gap between the pitch and the reality is the inflation. The people in Boxtown and Whitehaven get nothing from the distinction — the air is the same whether the turbines are powering Grok’s training or Anthropic’s inference, and the tactics used to build the thing never depended on the demand thesis being true. They only depended on overcoming the neighbors.

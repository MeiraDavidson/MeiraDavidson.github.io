What the Air Is Like

Noise gets the news coverage. The air does more of the lasting damage, and it arrives for a reason that has nothing to do with malice and everything to do with timing.

Data centers need backup power, because their tenants can’t tolerate even a few seconds of downtime. For years that meant diesel generators that ran rarely — a test now and then, the occasional outage — so the air impact was real but small. What changed is speed. The new AI campuses are too big for the existing grid to power on any normal schedule: expanding the grid for a major new load can take five to ten years, and these facilities are going up in two. The gap gets filled with on-site combustion, increasingly gas turbines, which install fast and, unlike backup generators, can run for months at a stretch.

The exhaust lands on whoever lives next door, and the pollutants are the familiar ones. Nitrogen oxides, which form smog and track with childhood asthma. Fine particulate matter — the PM2.5 linked to heart disease, stroke, lung cancer, and early death, with no level considered safe. Carbon monoxide. Formaldehyde and benzene, both carcinogens. Sulfur dioxide. Run enough turbines continuously in a residential area, and neighborhood levels can climb toward or past the limits the EPA sets, depending on weather, distance, and how many are running. Memphis is the most public case, but not the only one; across the country, new facilities are coming online with substantial on-site generation — some permitted, some running for years under “temporary” exemptions, some unpermitted and now in court.

How big does this get? A 2025 study modeled the public-health toll of U.S. data centers on their current path and projected that by 2030, the associated air pollution could be tied to roughly 600,000 asthma-symptom cases and about 1,300 premature deaths a year, with a health burden north of $20 billion annually — landing, disproportionately, on the host neighborhoods.

Those are projections, and they could move either way: worse if more capacity comes on combustion power, better if courts and regulators tighten up. The exact figure isn’t the point. The order of magnitude is — hundreds of thousands of asthma cases, thousands of early deaths, tens of billions in health costs, concentrated in specific places — and it is a burden the people bearing it never got to vote on. Data center air pollution isn’t yet at the scale of power generation or traffic. It is already at a scale that wouldn’t be tolerated if it were settling over different neighborhoods.

The Smaller Moves

Around these big levers sits a thicker layer of smaller tactics, used selectively. A few worth knowing by name, because once you can see them they stop feeling like the natural weather of “how development works.”

The boundary site: a parcel placed right on the line between two jurisdictions, so the operation sits in one while the noise and traffic fall on residents of the other — residents who can’t vote out the officials who approved it. The “temporary” label: the move at the center of the Memphis dispute from the energy chapter, where equipment that regulations exempt as temporary stays in place far longer than “temporary” was ever meant to cover.

Manufactured urgency: a hard “approve now or we build elsewhere” deadline, usually more movable than it sounds, designed to force a decision before residents can organize. The document dump: hundreds of pages filed days before a hearing, so no one has time to mount a real objection. And in Hood County, Texas, when officials floated a six-month pause to study a project, a state senator urged the attorney general to step in and block the moratorium — a reminder that even the modest tool of slowing down can be contested.

None of these is exotic. They’re the ordinary furniture of land development, refined over decades across many industries. What makes them matter here is the scale of what’s being built and the speed at which it’s arriving.

The Real Story Is the Asymmetry

Step back from the individual moves and the actual subject of this chapter comes into view, and it isn’t any single tactic. It’s the imbalance.

Defenders of the industry make a fair point: none of these tools is unique to data centers, and singling the industry out is, in a sense, unfair. That’s true. NDAs, shell companies, exclusionary-zoning suits — they predate this boom by decades and show up in every kind of large deal. But “common” describes how widespread a practice has become, not whether it’s fair to the people on the receiving end of it. The Grossers did not experience the gag order as standard procedure. They experienced it as being asked to sell their life’s work to someone who wouldn’t say their name.

Any one of these moves, a well-resourced community could probably counter. The trouble is that they don’t come one at a time. Combine half a dozen in a single project — the secret approach, the shell company, the quiet land assembly, the pre-emption bill, the lawsuit, the subsidy that divides the town — against a few volunteers reading municipal code after their day jobs, and the outcome is mostly settled before the public hearing ever happens. Run the same play across a whole region, by an industry that has been doing exactly this for a decade, and you get what we have now: data centers going up, over local objection, in the kinds of places least equipped to say no.

Naming the moves is most of what this chapter set out to do. Whether you end up finding them defensible or not, they are far easier to think about as a recognizable set of plays than as the vague, inevitable-seeming texture of progress arriving in your town.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. A ten-million-dollar offer, a non-disclosure agreement, and a buyer who will not say who they are. Dr Grosser said no. What would you have needed to know to decide?
  2. The community only worked it out by digging through public records and finding a request for 2.2 gigawatts. Should a project that size be discoverable only by accident?
  3. A professional team that does this for a living against a few volunteers reading municipal code after work. Is that a fair fight? What would make it fairer?

The Legal Weapons

How These Deals Actually Get Done

On a March afternoon in 2025, in Mason County, Kentucky — a rural stretch along the Ohio River that once billed itself as the second-largest tobacco market in the world — Dr. Timothy Grosser and his son Andy sat across a table from three men who had come with an offer. Ten million dollars for the 250-acre farm the family had worked for nearly four decades. That was thirty-five times what Grosser had paid for the land in 1988, and well above anything else changing hands in the county.

There was a catch. The men wouldn’t say who was buying, or what they intended to build. First, the Grossers would have to sign a non-disclosure agreement.

They said no. “I have spent half my life on this farm,” Grosser told a local reporter. “I’m just not giving up my happiness and freedom for any amount of money.” Months later — after a neighbor who had signed let something slip, and after residents went digging through public records and found a request for 2.2 gigawatts of electricity, nearly double what the local utility generated for everyone else combined — the county finally pieced it together. A hyperscale data center, backed by a Fortune 100 company that, to this day, has not put its name on the project.

That is how a great many of these deals begin: not with a public proposal a community can weigh, but with a quiet offer, a gag order, and a company that doesn’t exist anywhere but on paper.

This chapter is about the rest of that playbook — the legal tools that let the buildout go forward over the objections of the people who will live beside it. I want to be clear about one thing up front: almost none of it is illegal. Most of it is ordinary commercial real-estate practice, the kind of thing that happens in every large land deal.

What’s different here is the combination — half a dozen of these tools deployed together, on a specific kind of project that a lot of communities would turn down if you asked them plainly — and the lopsidedness of the contest. On one side, firms and law offices that have run this play a hundred times. On the other, a part-time county board reading zoning code on a weeknight. It’s worth seeing the moves clearly, whichever side of one you happen to find yourself on.

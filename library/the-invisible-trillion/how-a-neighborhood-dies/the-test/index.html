The Invisible Trillion · How a Neighborhood Dies

The Test

Here is a test worth carrying — for data centers, and for every other industrial siting decision you’ll ever read about.

The legal limits, for noise and air and water and light, are not floors of what’s acceptable. They’re the most harm the industry could get political permission for, usually set before the harm was well understood and rarely revisited since. A noise reading under the ordinance, an emission inside the permit, a rate hike the commission signed off on — none of that is evidence the harm is acceptable. It’s evidence the harm is legal. Those are different things, and the gap between them is where most of this lives.

The test that cuts through it: take any siting decision and ask whether the people making it would accept the result next to their own homes.

You don’t have to pry into anyone’s private life to run it, because the Highway Map answers it in the aggregate. These facilities are not, as a rule, built in the wealthy places where the executives and officials and lawyers who shape these decisions tend to live. The endless low hum, the combustion exhaust near a school, the all-night lighting into a child’s window, the bill that climbs to pay for someone else’s infrastructure, the deal cut in secret with your own representatives gagged — these land on the working-class edge, not where the deciders chose to live.

The dark-sky fixtures that keep glare out of a bedroom exist, and get installed where the affected residents have clout. The closed-loop cooling that doesn’t hum exists. The rate structures that make a facility pay its own way exist. The technical fixes aren’t the obstacle. They get used when the people who’d benefit can insist, and skipped when those people can’t.

The test isn’t complicated, and the answers it returns are uncomfortable with some consistency. What the discomfort points to is structural, not personal: the people deciding what gets built are mostly not the people who have to live with it, and the tools in the last chapter are what hold that arrangement in place. It’s an observation about how the system is wired — and once you’ve made it, it’s hard to stop seeing.

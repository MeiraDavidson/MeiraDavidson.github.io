The Approval Gauntlet

The third room — really a sequence of rooms — is the permitting stack, the layered approval process that turns a plan into poured concrete. Each layer has its own officials, its own standard, its own scope: federal environmental review where federal land or money is involved; state review where states have their own version; utility-commission sign-off for grid interconnection; water-authority approval; air permits, where the Clean Air Act’s line between “major source” and “temporary equipment” is exactly what the Memphis turbine fight turned on; then local zoning — the layer closest to residents and the one where opposition has historically worked best; then the largely ministerial building permits.

The part worth understanding is that the order is the developer’s choice, and it’s chosen for leverage. Approach a small town first, and it knows that saying no means forfeiting the tax base. Approach the utility first, lock in interconnection, then go to the town, and now a no means stranding commitments that are already approved. Sequenced well, the process produces approvals that look procedurally clean while being substantively settled in advance. By the time the most open layer — local zoning, where anyone can show up and speak — is reached, the prior layers have made refusal expensive, and the developer can wave those commitments at the local board.

So procedurally, almost no one is excluded; everyone has standing to comment somewhere. Effectively, most of the public is excluded most of the time, because participating across the whole stack takes technical knowledge, legal capacity, time, and persistence that ordinary residents don’t have. The gauntlet is open in principle and closed in practice.

And when it fails — when a town learns what’s coming too early, or organizes well, or votes no anyway — there’s a backup, the legal weaponry the earlier chapter detailed: the suit against the town, the state pre-emption bill, the eminent-domain claim, the move from local courts where opponents have standing to federal ones where they often don’t. Its real power is the threat. A town that knows it might be sued for blocking a project thinks twice even on good grounds it would probably win, because the legal fees alone could exceed its budget. The weaponry mostly works by shaping the choices the formal decision-makers face, long before any lawyer files anything.

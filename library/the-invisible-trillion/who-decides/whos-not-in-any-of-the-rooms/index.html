Who’s Not in Any of the Rooms

Turn the mechanisms inside out and the same names keep coming up as the parties who bear the consequences without a seat.

Residents near proposed infrastructure: the NDA keeps them ignorant while the decisions are live, the permitting stack lets them speak only after the leverage is gone, and the legal weaponry punishes their representatives for organizing well. Ratepayers across an affected utility region: the capital flows in, the grid expansion gets approved, and the costs are spread across them in commission proceedings most never knew were happening — the rate increases earlier chapters tallied, 94 percent in D.C., 74 in Maryland, 73 in Maine, landed on people with no realistic way to participate. The beneficial owners of the institutional capital: their retirement savings fund the buildout through fiduciaries whose incentives may or may not match their own.

A few groups sit even further out. The training-data subjects — anyone who ever wrote anything online — contributed to the models by default, through a collection process that included consent in no meaningful sense; the lawsuits and rules are still catching up to a thing that already happened. The supply-chain workers — the miners, refinery hands, fab technicians, and e-waste processors who make the hardware physically possible — sit behind very different labor laws depending on where they are, and the difference shows up in their working conditions and their life expectancies.

The general public, which polls consistently more cautious about the pace of AI deployment than the companies setting it, has no mechanism keeping pace to turn that caution into a binding constraint. And future generations, who’ll live in the world these infrastructure and climate and capability decisions are shaping, are excluded by simple necessity — no one in any of the rooms represents them.

What Follows From This

The reason to lay the mechanisms out plainly isn’t despair. It’s the opposite. Decisions made in specific rooms, by specific people, under specific procedures, are decisions made under rules — and rules are built things, not natural ones. They can be opened, rewritten, contested. The people can be regulated, pressured, or replaced. None of this machinery is a law of physics; it’s constructed, and constructed things are vulnerable to construction.

History bears that out. The environmental movement of the 1970s changed corporate behavior by changing the procedural rules that governed pollution decisions. The civil rights movement changed the law by changing who was in the room. The labor movement changed working conditions by changing the mechanisms through which labor disputes could be raised. None were inevitable; each required sustained attention from people who weren’t, at the time, the ones in charge.

What’s distinctive about this moment is the combination: the decisions are unusually large, the number of people making them is unusually small, the mechanisms are opaque to most outsiders, and the public is mostly not watching. The mechanisms run on that inattention — the NDA on constituents not asking in time, the financial machinery on no one separating performance from narrative, the permitting stack on residents skipping the early layers where leverage lives.

That’s also the seam in them. It is genuinely hard to predict whether attention arrives in time, and the honest answer is that it usually arrives only once the costs are visible enough to force it. But the rooms are named, the procedures are public, and the rules were written by people. The next chapter is about what that leaves open.

The Invisible Trillion · The Bill

The Railroad

Musk, to his credit, named the historical comparison himself. In SpaceX’s pre-IPO pitch, he likened the company to Union Pacific — the transcontinental railroad of the 1860s and ’70s, the project that physically stitched the American economy together. The implied argument is seductive: trust the trajectory, never mind the present losses, because the transformation will be vast enough to redeem the bet. And as history, it’s accurate — more accurate, maybe, than he intended.

Because the honest story of Union Pacific is not a clean story. The railroad was the site of one of the largest corruption scandals in American history. Its own insiders set up a separate construction company, Crédit Mobilier, awarded it wildly inflated contracts paid for with federal subsidies, and handed shares to members of Congress to keep the favorable legislation coming. Early investors were largely wiped out in the company’s first decade. The path to durable profit ran through bankruptcies, restructurings, federal bailouts, and political careers destroyed. The communities along the route were transformed, often violently — Native populations displaced, and thousands of Chinese laborers worked to death laying the track.

And here is the thing: the transformation was still real. The railroad did eventually become profitable. It did remake the economy, more or less as its boosters promised. Both halves are true at once — the project justified itself in the long run, and the specific deals that financed it were a machine for moving wealth from the public and from retail investors to the insiders who structured them. The transformation was real. The cost was real. The people who paid the cost were, by and large, not the people who collected the returns.

That is the template. The eventual technology may well justify some version of this buildout. It is far less likely to justify the specific transactions, the specific siting, and the specific cost allocation we’re watching in 2026. The gap between the project’s long-run worth and the present deal’s specific costs is the inflation — and the inflation is being absorbed, in the main, by the host communities, the ratepayers, and the late investors buying in near the top.

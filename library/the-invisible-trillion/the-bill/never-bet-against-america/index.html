The Invisible Trillion · The Bill

Never Bet Against America

There’s a line Warren Buffett has repeated across sixty years in public life: Never bet against America. He has closed crisis-year letters with it. It anchored him through the 1973 oil shock, the 1987 crash, the dotcom collapse, and 2008. And it has, every single time, eventually been right. The country absorbed the losses. The institutions restructured. The technology compounded. The next generation built on what the last one overbuilt — often on the very infrastructure the bubble had left behind.

I want to end on that line, because at the level of the country and the century, I think it’s correct. The AI buildout will almost certainly leave real, durable infrastructure. The technology will almost certainly produce real, durable gains. The security objectives will, in some form, be served. The country will, in some form, absorb the losses and move on — writing down what needs writing down, restructuring what needs restructuring, the way it always has. In 2080, the historians will likely look back on the 2020s as a period of enormous transformation, at enormous cost, that America came through in characteristic fashion.

But the qualifications matter, because they’re where the people reading this actually live, and the load-bearing word in Buffett’s line is eventually.

The country recovers eventually. The recoveries take time. The Dow’s 1929 peak was not recovered, in real inflation-adjusted terms, until 1954 — twenty-five years. The Nasdaq’s 2000 peak took fifteen years. Japan’s Nikkei peaked in 1989 and did not return to that level until 2024 — thirty-five years; an investor who bought the top in their forties was in their late seventies before their money came back, if they lived to see it. National resilience is real across generations. It is not a promise to any particular person on any particular timeline.

And “America” is a unit measured at the scale of the whole. The country compounds; specific people absorb specific losses, and many of them never personally recover. The retiree drawing down a crushed portfolio in 2009 was a real person whose story didn’t recover on the country’s schedule. That’s the gap Buffett’s line quietly contains: the recovery’s timing and the original capital’s fate are two different stories.

The infrastructure survives the wreck — the dark fiber of 1999 carried the streaming boom a decade later, the over-laid railroads of the 1870s carried the next century’s industry — but in every case the original equity holders were wiped out and the eventual owners bought the assets for cents on the dollar from the ruins. The infrastructure survived. The capital that built it did not.

So Buffett is right, and he is the kind of right that should be read carefully. The country recovers, the infrastructure persists, the long arc bends upward. The affected retirees will not, in the main, be made whole by that arc. The host communities will not be repaired by the eventual repurposing of the facilities that hollowed them out. The bill is being presented, in 2026, to people who did not order what’s being charged to them — and the long-run resilience of the American economy, real as it is, is not the same thing as a refund.

Questions to talk about

  1. Two defences: the smart money has spoken, and it is a matter of national survival. The author calls both serious and partly right. State the strongest version of each.

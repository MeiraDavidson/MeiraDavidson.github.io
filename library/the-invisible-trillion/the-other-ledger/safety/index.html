Safety

The list of things that don’t kill you because a chip is watching is long.

Car airbags deploy because a small chip — an accelerometer, the same technology as the one in your phone — detects a crash and fires the airbag in a few thousandths of a second. Anti-lock brakes work because a chip monitors each wheel’s speed fifty times a second and adjusts brake pressure to prevent skidding. Electronic stability control, now mandatory in most countries, works the same way. Traffic light timing, which reduces accidents and congestion, is handled by chip-based controllers that coordinate across whole cities. The lines on modern highways that buzz when you drift across them — chip-activated. Commercial aircraft autopilot, which has made flying safer per mile than almost any other form of transportation — chip-dependent.

Earthquake early warning systems, which exist in Japan, Mexico, California, and a few other places, use networks of chip-based seismometers to detect the initial shock wave of an earthquake and send warnings to cities that haven’t felt it yet. The warnings arrive seconds to tens of seconds before the shaking. It sounds small. It has saved a lot of lives. Trains are stopped automatically. Surgeons pause. People brace.

Tsunami warning systems, wildfire detection, hurricane tracking, flood prediction — all of these run on chip-based sensor networks feeding into data centers that run prediction models. Weather forecasting accuracy has roughly doubled every 30 years since 1950, and the doubling is driven primarily by compute: bigger models, more data, more detailed simulations of the atmosphere. A five-day forecast in 2026 is about as accurate as a one-day forecast was in 1970. A reliable three-day hurricane track forecast in 2026 means cities evacuate with enough lead time to actually get people out. In 1970 it didn’t, and hurricanes killed far more people.

Hospital monitoring systems alarm when a patient’s vitals cross a threshold. Continuous glucose monitors for diabetics warn of low blood sugar before symptoms. Home smoke detectors that networked into chip-based hubs now detect slow-smoldering fires hours earlier than the old standalone ones.

Each of these is small on its own. Together, they are one of the largest quiet revolutions in how humans die, which is to say: we die less, and later, and less randomly, than our grandparents did, and a surprising fraction of the reason is that chips are constantly watching for the things that would hurt us.

The Invisible Trillion · The Other Ledger

Access

There is a story about the second half of the 20th century that goes like this: rich countries invented things, poor countries eventually got them, but always later and always more expensively, because the infrastructure of the rich countries was what the things depended on.

In the 21st century, that story has broken in a specific place, which is mobile technology. A poor country does not need to build out the twentieth-century infrastructure (landline phones, bank branches, physical retail, physical libraries) to get most of the benefits. It can skip straight to the twenty-first. And it has.

In Kenya, most adults have a bank account — not because Kenya built out a bank branch network, but because Kenya pioneered mobile payments through a system called M-Pesa, running on chips in cell phones. People in rural Kenya who previously had no way to save money or transfer it to family in other villages now have, essentially, a bank in their pocket. The consequences have been enormous; multiple studies have shown measurable reductions in poverty tied to M-Pesa adoption.

In India, Aadhaar — a national biometric ID system — has given a billion-plus people formal identification for the first time in their lives. Aadhaar has plenty of problems (privacy concerns, exclusion errors, misuse), and critics have documented them thoroughly. It has also given hundreds of millions of poor people the ability to receive government benefits, open bank accounts, and access services they could not previously access.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, millions of children learn to read on cheap tablets running free educational apps. The tablets have chips made in fabs those children will never see, loaded with software written by engineers they will never meet, distributing curriculum prepared by teachers they will never talk to. The children learn anyway. The outcomes, as measured by standardized literacy tests, are not as good as a great classroom teacher, and they are much better than no classroom teacher, which is the actual alternative in a lot of places.

Accessibility technology for people with disabilities has, in the last 20 years, transitioned from “specialized, expensive, often bad” to “built into every phone and laptop.” Screen readers for the blind, captions for the deaf, dictation software for people who can’t type, eye-tracking input for people who can’t use their hands, hearing aids that connect directly to phones and are much cheaper than the old specialized ones, navigation systems for people with visual impairments. These are not specialized products anymore; they are features you can turn on in your phone. A blind person with a 2026 smartphone has access to a set of capabilities that a blind person in 1990 could not have dreamed of.

Translation has gone, in the last decade, from “often garbled machine translation, useful for the gist” to “good enough that two people speaking different languages can have a real conversation through a phone.” This is a small-looking change with surprisingly large consequences. Travel is different. Immigration is different. Scientific collaboration across languages is different. A Kenyan farmer and a Finnish engineer can now, routinely, read each other’s text in their own languages, in real time.

These are goods. Some of them are extraordinary goods. None of them are perfect. All of them depend, at the bottom, on the chip-and-data-center infrastructure this book is about.

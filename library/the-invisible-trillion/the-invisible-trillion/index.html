The Invisible Trillion

The Invisible Trillion

Why You Have Strong Opinions About a Thing You’ve Never Seen

Look at the phone closest to you. Pick it up if you can.

The chip at the center of that phone — the one doing almost all of the actual thinking — is about the size of a fingernail. Inside that fingernail-sized chip are somewhere between ten and twenty billion transistors, depending on which phone you have.

Ten billion is a number that does not mean anything to humans. Our brains did not evolve for it. So here are some numbers it equals, to give you a feel:

  • The number of people on Earth, times one and a half.
  • The number of heartbeats in three average human lifetimes.
  • The number of seconds in about 300 years.

All of that, in your pocket. Working right now. Perfectly. Every one of those transistors switching billions of times per second, every single one of them smaller than a virus.

Now zoom out. There are a few billion smartphones on Earth, each with a chip like this one. There are also computers, tablets, cars (the newest cars have around 1,500 chips each), traffic lights, hospital monitors, game consoles, satellites, refrigerators, dishwashers, and — importantly for the rest of this book — a few tens of millions of specialized chips in data centers. All told, humans have manufactured, cumulatively since the 1960s, somewhere around ten sextillion transistors.

A sextillion is a one followed by 21 zeros. Ten sextillion is a ten followed by 21 zeros.

For comparison: the number of grains of sand on every beach on Earth is somewhere around seven and a half quintillion — a seven and a half followed by 18 zeros. We have made, by our best estimates, more than a thousand transistors for every grain of beach sand on Earth. And we make about a trillion more every second.

This is not a metaphor. This is arithmetic.

The Most Impressive Thing You’ve Never Looked At

Here is a fact that took me a long time to really absorb, and which might be worth pausing on.

Of all the objects humans make — buildings, cars, planes, bridges, satellites, particle accelerators — the most precisely manufactured thing, by a wide margin, is a modern computer chip.

Not somewhat more precise. Not 10% more precise. Thousands of times more precise than anything else we make.

The features on a current high-end chip are about 3 nanometers across. A nanometer is a billionth of a meter. Three nanometers is roughly fifteen silicon atoms in a row. If you enlarged one of these transistors to the size of a grain of rice, the chip it came from would be about the size of Manhattan. Every single one of the fifteen billion transistors on that Manhattan-sized chip would have to be placed correctly, wired correctly, and working correctly, or the chip doesn’t work at all.

We make a trillion of these a year.

The factories that make them — “fabs,” short for fabrication plants — are the most expensive buildings humans have ever built for their size. A new one costs around $20 billion, which is about twice what it cost to build the tallest building in the world. Inside the fab, the air is about a thousand times cleaner than in a hospital operating room, because a single dust particle landing on a wafer in the wrong place ruins the chip. Workers wear suits that make them look like astronauts, except the suits are to protect the chips from the humans, not the humans from the chips.

The machines inside the fab that actually draw the transistors onto the silicon are, themselves, the most complicated machines humans have ever built. One of them — called an EUV lithography machine — contains 100,000 precisely-machined parts, costs around $350 million, weighs 180 tons, and is built by exactly one company in the world, in the Netherlands, that has a decade-long waiting list. These machines shoot lasers at drops of molten tin to produce a specific color of light that doesn’t exist anywhere in nature, and they use that light to draw the features on your phone’s chip.

All of this exists. None of it is science fiction. It is all happening, somewhere, right now, as you read this sentence.

And almost no one has ever seen it.

One Last Thing

Before you turn the page, I want you to look at your phone again.

Feel the weight of it. Notice the screen, which is made of chemically-strengthened glass that didn’t exist twenty years ago. Notice the case, which has rare earth metals in it that were mined on three continents. Notice the little camera, which has a chip in it that can capture more detail than your eye can see. Notice the battery, which contains lithium that was probably evaporated out of saltwater in the Atacama Desert.

Now notice that this whole object — this genuinely astonishing object, maybe the most complex thing a person is likely to own in their lifetime — exists because of a global supply chain involving roughly two million people, seventy countries, and some of the most precisely engineered machines ever built.

You bought it for $800, or your parents did, or someone handed it to you used. You have probably dropped it at least once. You use it mostly to watch videos and text your friends.

None of this is a criticism. I use my phone to watch videos and text my friends too. The point is just that something incredibly strange is happening: we have become, in the space of a couple of generations, casual owners of technology so complex that no single person on Earth fully understands all of it. The chip in your phone was designed by one set of humans, manufactured by another, assembled by another, and programmed by another, and none of them know what any of the others do in detail. The whole thing only works because every link in the chain does its job and trusts the other links.

This is worth thinking about. Because the next time someone tells you, with great confidence, what the “truth” about AI or data centers or chips is — including me, in this book — you should remember that the thing they are confidently describing is a thing almost no one fully understands, and which no single person has seen all of, and which is growing faster than our ability to keep up with it.

Humility, in this domain, is the correct starting point.

Let’s begin.

A note on sources: the transistor count figures are from SEMI industry reports and estimates of cumulative chip production since the first commercial integrated circuit in 1961; the “more transistors than grains of sand” claim is widely repeated but the exact ratio depends on how you count sand, so I have used conservative estimates in both directions. The fab cost figures are from public disclosures by TSMC, Samsung, and Intel. The EUV lithography details are from ASML, the Dutch company that makes the machines. The Manhattan-to-rice-grain scale comparison is my own, done the back-of-envelope way, and you should feel free to check it.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Ten or twenty billion transistors in a fingernail. The author gives three comparisons because the number means nothing on its own. Invent a fourth that would work for someone your age.
  2. He notes the 'more transistors than grains of sand' claim is widely repeated but depends on how you count sand, so he used conservative estimates both ways. Why tell the reader that rather than just using the striking version?
  3. 'Why You Have Strong Opinions About a Thing You've Never Seen.' Name something else you hold an opinion about and have never seen. Where did the opinion come from?

A Short Map of What’s Coming

The rest of the book is organized like this.

The next three chapters are about how chips actually work and get made. This is the part that will probably be most new to you, and the most fun. You are going to learn about sand and ingots and photolithography and why Taiwan is geopolitically the most important island on Earth. If you don’t care about any of that yet, you will by the end of Chapter 4.

Then there are three chapters about the real costs. Energy, water, and mining/e-waste. Each of them follows the same structure: here are the numbers, here is what’s actually a problem, here is what’s been exaggerated, here is what we don’t know. I tried to be careful about not soft-pedaling, because you can tell when you’re being soft-pedaled to, and also not alarmist, because the alarmism version of this story is already everywhere and you don’t need more of it.

Then there is a chapter about how this all compares to the last three hundred years of human history, which is maybe the most important chapter in the book and which also happens to be my favorite.

Then there is a chapter about the other side of the ledger — what these chips and data centers and AI systems actually do that is good. (Yes, really. Yes, it’s a lot.) This is the chapter that will bore people who wanted the book to be about how bad technology is, and it’s the chapter that will bore people who wanted the book to be about how great technology is, which is probably a sign it’s doing its job.

Then there is a chapter about something almost nobody talks about, which is that the scarce resource in this whole system isn’t the electricity or the water or the silicon. It’s your attention. And the attention economy that’s been built around the chips in your phone is, in a lot of ways, more environmentally and psychologically expensive than all the training runs put together. That chapter might make you mad. That’s fine.

Then two closing chapters: one about who actually decides what happens in this industry — which is a surprisingly small number of people, making decisions you should probably know about — and one about what you can actually do that would actually matter, which is also a surprisingly small list, and does not include turning off your wifi router.

Then the book ends. Take what you want from it.

The Invisibility Problem

Here is what’s strange about all of this.

If you grew up in 1925, the most impressive machines in your life were visible. The ocean liners were enormous. The locomotives shook the ground. The dams were on the news. The factories had smokestacks you could see from ten miles away. When your grandmother talked about “modern industry,” she was pointing at something: it was there, it was big, it moved and it smoked.

If you grew up in 2012, the most impressive machines in your life were invisible. They were in a building in Oregon, or Ireland, or Singapore, that you have never been to and will never be to. The building does not have a smokestack. It has no windows. It does not move. Most of it is not even labeled on the outside. You cannot take a field trip there, not because it’s secret, but because there is nothing to see — just rows of identical black rectangles in a cold room, humming.

The chips themselves are smaller than your thumbnail. They are sealed inside plastic cases inside bigger metal cases inside cloud racks inside buildings you don’t know the location of. The whole stack — from the atoms in the silicon up to the server farm you’re talking to when you open an app — is completely and totally invisible.

This matters more than it seems to.

Humans evolved to form opinions about things we can see and smell and hear. Our intuitions about what is powerful, what is dangerous, what is polluting, what is wasteful, what is precious — all of those intuitions were calibrated in a world where the stuff we were judging was right in front of us. A smoking factory looks bad. A concrete dam looks powerful. A pile of garbage looks wasteful. A river on fire — which was an actual thing in the United States in 1969 — looks like something you should do something about.

A data center looks like a beige warehouse.

The chip in your phone looks like nothing. It looks like a smooth black rectangle in a plastic shell inside a metal case inside your pocket. It is doing more computation per second than every computer in the world combined could do in 1985, and it looks like nothing.

This is why the arguments about technology — about AI, about data centers, about energy, about privacy, about everything this book is going to get into — are so weirdly disconnected from reality. We are arguing about things we cannot see. We have no physical intuitions about them. We have no grandmother-pointing-at-the-factory version of how to think about them. So the arguments fill up with feelings and vibes and TikTok videos, because feelings and vibes and TikTok videos are what we have when we don’t have direct experience.

The first job of this book is just to get you to see some of this stuff. Not literally — you’re not going to tour a fab, probably, unless you grow up to work in one. But to build up the mental images, the scale comparisons, the physical intuitions that you would have had if this whole industry were visible. Once you have those, the arguments stop being about vibes and start being about something real.

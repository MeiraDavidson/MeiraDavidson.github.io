Why This Book Is Worth Reading

You already have opinions about the stuff in this book.

You have opinions about AI. You have opinions about data centers. Probably you have opinions about social media, and about screen time, and about whether “Big Tech” is good or bad for the world. These opinions came from somewhere — from TikTok, from your friends, from a documentary, from a teacher, from a parent. They might be right. They might be completely wrong. You don’t actually know, and that’s not your fault, because the stuff these opinions are about is invisible, and you’ve been asked to have opinions about it anyway.

Here is what I am going to ask of you for the next ten chapters.

I am going to ask you to hold those opinions a little more loosely than usual. Not abandon them. Not pretend you don’t have them. Just — loosen your grip. Look at the actual numbers, the actual physics, the actual tradeoffs. See what survives contact with reality.

Some of your opinions will survive. Some of them will probably change. I don’t know which ones, and neither do you, yet. That’s the point.

I am also going to make you a promise in return. I am not going to pretend things are okay when they are not. If the water use is a real problem, I am going to tell you the water use is a real problem. If the mining is a moral disaster, I am going to tell you the mining is a moral disaster. If the companies are being dishonest about their emissions, I am going to say they are being dishonest about their emissions. You have probably already encountered, in your life, adults who say “don’t worry, it’s fine” about things that are not fine. I am not going to be one of them. The whole point of the book is to tell you what is actually going on, including the parts where what’s going on is genuinely bad.

The deal, in other words: I take your worries seriously and give you the real numbers, and in exchange you give me ten chapters before you conclude anything.

Fair?

How the Lights Stay On · The Grid Grows a Brain

The Grid Grows a Brain, part 2

You probably have one. About three-quarters of American households do, as of 2026.

A smart meter is the small device, usually mounted on the outside wall of your house near where the electric service comes in, that has replaced the old analog spinning-wheel meter. It measures your electricity consumption every 15 minutes to one hour and reports the readings back to the utility automatically over wireless or power-line communications.

Smart meters do three important things.

First, they let the utility see, in real time, exactly which houses are using how much electricity. When a tree branch falls on a line and your block goes dark, the utility now knows immediately, without anyone calling. This shortens outage response times dramatically.

Second, they enable time-of-use pricing. Instead of paying a flat rate per kilowatt-hour all day, your bill can vary based on when you used the electricity — cheap at midnight, expensive at 6 PM. This gives the utility a tool to shift demand off the peak hours when the grid is most stressed, and it gives you a tool to save money by doing your laundry overnight or programming your dishwasher to start at 2 AM.

Third, they let the grid talk to your house.

That last one is the most important, and we will spend the rest of the chapter on it.

Here is the most important shift in modern grid management.

For a hundred years, the grid had one tool: it could ramp generation up and down to match demand. Need more power at 6 PM? Run more gas turbines. Less power at 3 AM? Take some plants offline. The grid worked supply.

The smart grid added a second tool: it could also flex demand to match generation. Need less power at 6 PM? Pay customers to use less. Got too much solar at noon? Pay customers to use more. Suddenly the grid had two ways to balance, not one.

This is called demand response, and it is one of the most powerful and least understood tools in modern grid management.

Demand response works because most electricity use is somewhat flexible if you give people the right signal. Air conditioning does not have to run at exactly 100 percent at 5:30 PM — the house can coast for fifteen minutes at a slightly higher temperature without anyone noticing. Water heaters do not have to run on a fixed schedule — they can heat up at midnight when the grid is sleepy and hold the heat. Electric cars do not have to charge the moment you plug in — they can wait until the cheapest hour of the night.

In practice, demand response programs work in two main ways.

In a price-based program, your utility charges you different rates at different times. You see the rates, you adjust your behavior, the grid benefits indirectly. This is the simplest version.

In a direct-control program, you give the utility permission to nudge your devices on your behalf. A smart thermostat. A smart water heater. A smart EV charger. The utility sends a signal that says the grid is stressed for the next hour — please cycle off. Your house cooperates automatically. You may not even notice. At the end of the month, you get a small credit on your bill for participating.

The number of demand-response programs in the United States has more than doubled in the last five years. Most large utilities now have at least one. The collective load they can shift — what engineers call “controllable load” — is growing into the tens of gigawatts nationally. That is, the entire grid’s daily peak can be moved by tens of gigawatts, just by sending signals to enough smart devices.

This is real, and it is happening now. Your refrigerator is starting to talk to the grid. The grid is starting to talk back.

Once homes have smart meters and demand-response devices, the next shift becomes possible: homes that not only consume electricity but also produce it.

A house with rooftop solar generates electricity during the day. Some of it the family uses; some of it flows back to the grid. A house with a battery (Chapter 7) can store excess solar from noon and discharge it during the evening peak. An electric car parked in the driveway can, with the right hardware, charge from the grid when prices are low and feed back to the grid when prices are high.

These small generators and storage devices, scattered across millions of homes, are called distributed energy resources, or DERs.

In the old grid, the distribution network — the wires from the substation to your house — was a one-way pipe. Power flowed out to customers. Period.

In the new grid, the distribution network is becoming a marketplace. Power flows in both directions. The local transformer (Chapter 5) sometimes pushes power down to houses, sometimes accepts power back up from houses, often in the same hour. The wires were not originally designed for this. The local utilities are upgrading them — sometimes faster than they would like, sometimes slower than the homeowners would like — to handle the new bi-directional flow.

When you coordinate enough DERs across enough homes, something remarkable becomes possible.

If you have ten thousand Tesla Powerwalls scattered across ten thousand California homes, you have a strange asset. Individually, each Powerwall stores 13.5 kilowatt-hours of electricity. Collectively, they store 135 megawatt-hours. If you could discharge them all at once, you could deliver 50 or 60 megawatts of power for two or three hours — equivalent to a medium-sized power plant.

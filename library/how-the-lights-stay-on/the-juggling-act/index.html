How the Lights Stay On

Chapter 2: The Juggling Act

In a low building tucked behind a fence in Audubon, Pennsylvania, there is a room with no windows.

Inside the room, a wall of screens stretches from floor to ceiling. Lines of color trace across them, climbing and falling. Numbers blink. Maps of the eastern United States glow in different shades of green and yellow and the occasional red. There are desks in front of the wall, and at each desk a person sits in a swivel chair, watching, sometimes typing, sometimes picking up a phone.

This is one of the most important rooms in America, and almost no one outside the electricity industry knows it exists.

It is the control room of PJM Interconnection — the company that manages the flow of electricity for sixty-five million people across thirteen states and the District of Columbia, from northern Illinois to North Carolina. New York has its own grid operator. New England has another. California has another. Texas has its own. Together, a small number of these control rooms keep the lights on for the entire United States.

They never close. There is always someone in the chair. Day shift, swing shift, graveyard shift. Christmas morning, the middle of a hurricane, the early hours of a new year. The grid never sleeps, so the operators never sleep.

What are they watching?

Mostly, they are watching one number.

The number is 60.000.

It is the frequency of the electricity flowing through every wire in North America. The current in your wall is not flowing in one direction — it is alternating, swinging back and forth, sixty times a second. Every appliance you own is built to run on that exact rhythm. Plug a hair dryer designed for sixty cycles per second into a European outlet that runs at fifty, and the hair dryer will not work right. The motor will spin at the wrong speed. The clock will lose time.

That number — 60 hertz, or 60 Hz — is the heartbeat of the American grid. Every generator from Maine to Florida, and across most of Texas, is spinning in time with that beat. The huge steam turbines in coal and nuclear plants. The hydroelectric turbines under dams. The gas turbines that ramp up on hot afternoons. They are not just spinning fast. They are spinning in lockstep. All of them. At the same beat. Always.

If you could stand somewhere outside the planet and watch the eastern half of the United States as a single object, you would see thousands of giant rotating machines — turbines the size of trucks, generators the weight of houses — all moving together, as if one invisible hand were keeping time. That is what the grid is.

This is one of the most extraordinary engineering achievements in human history. And the room with no windows in Pennsylvania exists to keep it that way.

Here is why the number matters.

When demand exceeds supply — when more people are drawing electricity than the generators are producing — the generators slow down. Not by much. A fraction of a percent. The number on the screens in Audubon drops to 59.98, then 59.97. The heartbeat is faltering.

When supply exceeds demand — when generators are producing more than people are using — the generators speed up. The number climbs to 60.02, then 60.04. The heartbeat is racing.

Frequency is the balance, made visible. It is the single most important measurement in the entire system, because it tells you, in real time, whether supply and demand are in balance. You don’t have to track every house and every power plant separately. You just have to watch the frequency. If it’s at 60.000, things are fine. If it’s drifting, somebody somewhere is having a problem.

The operators in Audubon are watching that number to four decimal places. They can see it drifting before the public would ever notice. And they have tools to bring it back.

Picture a juggler. She has six balls in the air. If she throws them all up at the same height and catches them at the same rhythm, the act looks easy. But if one ball comes down a little fast, she has to throw it back up a little harder. If a ball lags behind, she has to slow her hand to meet it. Every ball, every second. Forever.

The grid operator is juggling supply and demand. The “balls” are power plants — coal plants, gas plants, nuclear plants, hydro dams, wind farms, solar farms, batteries. The operator does not control demand directly; that depends on whether millions of people are turning on their air conditioners or their coffeemakers or their TVs. But the operator can adjust supply.

If frequency starts to drop, the operator calls for more generation. Not by phone — by software, which sends signals to power plants telling them to spin a little harder, generate a little more. This adjustment is called frequency regulation, and it happens automatically thousands of times a day. A computer system called Automatic Generation Control — AGC for short — handles the small adjustments in seconds. The operators handle the bigger ones, the weird ones, the ones the software is not sure about.

There is also something called spinning reserve. This is a power plant that is running, synced to the grid, burning a little fuel — but not actually generating much electricity. It is just there, hot and spinning, ready. The moment another power plant trips offline unexpectedly, the spinning reserve takes over within seconds. It is the grid’s safety net, and it costs real money to maintain. A reserve plant is being paid to do mostly nothing except be ready.

This whole system — the regulation, the reserves, the operator watching the number — exists because of the single fact we ended Chapter 1 with: electricity is made and used in the same instant. There is no warehouse. So the warehouse has to be replaced by vigilance.

Here is the part that, when I first understood it, genuinely changed the way I think about the country I live in.

Every generator in the Eastern Interconnection — that’s everything from Maine to Florida, west to the eastern Dakotas, and including parts of eastern Texas — is electromagnetically locked to every other generator. They are not just spinning at the same speed by coincidence. They are physically pulling each other along.

If one generator tries to slow down, the magnetic fields of all the others gently pull it back up to speed. If one tries to race ahead, the others gently hold it back. The whole thing is one giant machine, the size of half a continent, moving together. The biggest single machine humans have ever built.

There are three of these big machines in North America: the Eastern Interconnection, the Western Interconnection (everything west of the Rockies), and the Texas Interconnection (mostly the state of Texas, which famously chose to stay separate from the other two — that’s a story we’ll come back to). The three machines are not synchronized to each other. They are connected by special direct-current links that let power flow between them, but their rotations are independent.

When you flip on a light switch in Philadelphia, you are participating in this machine. Your lamp pulls a tiny amount of current from the wires in your wall, which pull from the lines on the street, which pull from the substation, which pull from the transmission lines, which pull, finally, from generators hundreds of miles away that — at that exact moment — spin a fractionally tinier bit harder to feed your bulb. The whole machine flexes around you, infinitesimally, for as long as the lamp is on.

This is happening for every appliance in every house in every state, every second of every day.

For most of the twentieth century, the synchronized machine had a useful property: it was heavy.

Steam turbines and hydroelectric turbines have enormous rotating mass. They store kinetic energy the way a freight train stores momentum. If something on the grid suddenly changes — a power plant trips, a transmission line drops — the spinning mass of all those generators provides a few seconds of buffer. They slow down a tiny bit, releasing their stored energy into the grid, while the operators scramble to bring in replacements. This buffer is called inertia, and for a hundred years it was free. You got it just by having heavy spinning things.

Solar panels and battery storage do not have rotating mass. They use electronics to put electricity on the grid, and they can mimic the heartbeat — but they do not provide inertia for free. As more of the grid becomes solar and batteries, the system becomes less heavy. Less forgiving. Faster to react, and faster to fail.

This is one of the genuinely unsolved engineering problems of the energy transition. Engineers are working on something called grid-forming inverters — software-defined electronics that can fake inertia and even help stabilize a grid without any spinning mass at all. The technology is real and improving fast. But it is not yet fully deployed, and the grid is not yet ready to run on solar and batteries alone.

This is why a chapter on storage is coming later in this book. And why a chapter on the future is coming after that. The juggling act is not getting any easier.

In the control room in Audubon, the day shift ends in the late afternoon. The operator who has been watching the number for eight hours stands up, stretches, and walks to a meeting room with the operator coming on for the swing shift. They go over what happened — a generator that came offline this morning, a line that has been running hot, a forecast of thunderstorms over Maryland. They drink coffee. They sign a logbook. Then the new operator walks out to the chair, sits down, looks up at the wall of screens, and watches the number.

60.001.

60.000.

59.998.

60.000.

You are reading this sentence in a country where, right now, somebody you have never met is watching that number, making sure your lights stay on. They are good at their job. They have been doing it for years. Most of them love what they do, even though almost nobody outside the industry knows they exist.

They are the jugglers. They will be back in this book when we get to August 14, 2003 — because on that day, in a different control room in Ohio, the juggling act came briefly and catastrophically apart.

But for now, the heartbeat is steady.

60.000.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The whole grid is watched through one number: 60.000. Why would a single number be enough to tell operators whether supply and demand are in balance?
  2. These control rooms never close — Christmas morning, the middle of a hurricane, the early hours of a new year. What kind of person takes that job, and what do we owe them?
  3. Almost nobody outside the industry knows these rooms exist. Make a list of other jobs that hold up daily life and are equally invisible to you.

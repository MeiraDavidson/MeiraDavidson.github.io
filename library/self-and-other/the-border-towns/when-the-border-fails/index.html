Self & Other · The Border Towns

When the Border Fails

The T cells in the gut are similarly unusual. A large fraction of them are not the killer T cells or helper T cells of Chapter 3, but a third class called regulatory T cells — we’ll meet them properly in Chapter 7 — whose entire job is to actively suppress immune responses. They walk around the gut wall calming everyone else down. Their cytokines say, in chemical language: this is fine. We’ve seen this before. Don’t escalate. Without them, the gut would be in permanent civil war, and we’d be permanently sick.

So the rule of the gut is not “attack everything that isn’t you.” The rule is something more like: “keep things where they belong, sample what’s happening, escalate only when something is genuinely out of place.”

This is a profoundly different posture from the one we described in earlier chapters. It is also, in evolutionary terms, the older posture. Animals were living with bacteria in their guts long before they had B cells and T cells. The system at the gut wall is, in some ways, the original immune system, with the spectacular adaptive machinery layered on top of it.

When the Border Fails

When the negotiation breaks down at the gut, the consequences are real.

If the tight junctions between enterocytes start to leak — if bacteria, or fragments of bacteria, start crossing the line in ways they shouldn’t — the immune system on the other side does what immune systems do. It mounts a response. The response can be acute, like food poisoning, or chronic, like the inflammatory bowel diseases we’ll meet in Chapter 10 (Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis), where the gut wall is in a long-running, low-grade war that does damage on both sides.

The phrase “leaky gut” gets thrown around a lot in popular health writing, often as the cause of everything from fatigue to mood disorders. The careful version of this idea is real: gut barrier integrity matters, and there are well-documented diseases in which it fails. The hype version of this idea — that you can fix any vague symptom by buying a supplement that “heals leaky gut” — is mostly not real. As with the microbiome, the underlying biology is genuinely interesting, and the marketing is mostly running ahead of the science. We’ll come back to this in Chapter 14, when we talk about what actually helps and what only sounds like it does.

What’s worth holding onto from this chapter is something more general. The border tissues of your body — skin, gut, lungs — are not just walls. They are negotiated zones, with their own immune systems, their own rules, and in the case of the gut, their own ecosystem of trillions of resident microbes that your body has decided, on the whole, to keep around.

The Old Friends

There’s one last thing to mention before we leave the borders, because it sets up a story that’s going to come back later in the book.

In the late 1980s, a British epidemiologist named David Strachan noticed something odd in the data on hay fever. Children from large families — lots of older siblings, lots of household chaos, lots of being sneezed on — got hay fever and asthma and eczema at much lower rates than children from small, clean households. The effect was strong and consistent. Something about being exposed to more microbial chaos, early in life, seemed to protect children from later allergies.

Strachan called this the hygiene hypothesis, and it spread quickly into popular culture, often in a garbled form. The garbled version says: kids today get sick from being too clean. Let your children play in dirt. Don’t bother with hand-washing. This is not what Strachan said and not what the data show.

The careful version of the idea, refined over the last thirty years and now usually called the old friends hypothesis, goes like this. The human immune system evolved over millions of years in the constant presence of certain microbes — not the dramatic ones that cause epidemics, but the boring background ones: soil bacteria, harmless gut companions, the parasites and microbes that lived alongside our ancestors in their farms and forests and dirty floors. The immune system, especially in early childhood, appears to need exposure to these “old friends” in order to learn the right calibration. Without them, the system seems to come up miscalibrated, more prone to attacking harmless things (allergies) and sometimes more prone to attacking the body’s own tissues (some autoimmune diseases).

The hygiene that matters — hand-washing during a flu outbreak, vaccinating against deadly diseases, sanitizing drinking water — is not the problem. The loss of contact with old friends is. Modern children grow up with much less exposure to soil, to farm animals, to certain harmless gut microbes that all human ancestors had. The connection to rising rates of allergy and autoimmune disease is one of the more provocative ideas in modern immunology. It is not fully proven. It is also not crazy.

What this means in practice is more subtle than “let your kids eat dirt.” It probably means something like: don’t be afraid of normal childhood mess, normal contact with pets, normal exposure to the outdoors, normal early-life microbial chaos. The immune system seems to learn from it. The benefit, if it’s real, is calibration — not toughness, not boosting, but better judgment.

So this is the country at the borders. A skin you shed weekly, an acidic surface that bacteria can sit on but rarely cross. A gut tube that is technically still outside, lined with cells one layer thick, sealed with proteins that mostly hold. Trillions of bacteria, mostly cooperative, kept politely in their lane by antibodies that don’t kill them. Regulatory T cells walking the wall, telling everyone to stay calm. Lungs that are folded surfaces of breathtaking delicacy, filtering twelve thousand liters of air a day.

Most of your immune system is here. Most of your interaction with the outside world is here. Most of what you’ll ever do, immunologically, is decide what to tolerate and what to refuse, on surfaces you cannot see.

We’ve now met all the major players. The innate first responders. The adaptive specialists. The memory cells. The border guards and the regulatory cells.

What we haven’t yet met is the system that holds them in check — the system that decides which things get attacked and which things get tolerated, the system whose failure is the subject of the second half of this book. That system has two main parts. One of them is a kind of molecular ID badge, worn by every cell in your body. The other is a school, brutal and beautiful, hidden in your chest, where your T cells go to learn what counts as you.

We’ll start with the badge.

Questions to talk about

  1. By cell count you are about half you and about half something else. Why are you not permanently inflamed?
  2. The once-famous 'ten times more bacteria than human cells' figure has been quietly walked back to roughly one-to-one. Why do corrected numbers travel so much more slowly than striking ones?

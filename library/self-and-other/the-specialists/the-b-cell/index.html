The B Cell

When he ran his experiment and looked at the results, he saw exactly that.

The DNA inside an antibody-making cell was not the same as the DNA in the rest of the body. Pieces had been deleted. Other pieces had been stuck together. Whole stretches of the chromosome had been cut, shuffled, and resealed — like someone had taken a manuscript, sliced it into a hundred pieces, picked a few at random, and glued them back together to make a new sentence.

Each B cell, it turned out, did this individually, when it was first being made in the bone marrow. The cell would take three large libraries of gene fragments — biologists named them V, D, and J — and pick one fragment at random from each library. Then it would splice them together. The combination of these three fragments became the recipe for that cell’s particular antibody. Different cell, different random combination, different antibody.

That was the basic move. But it got more elaborate. The splicing wasn’t always clean — sometimes a few extra DNA letters were added at the joints, or a few were chewed off, generating even more variation. Then, once a B cell had made its initial antibody and gotten activated by a real threat, it would start mutating its antibody genes deliberately, refining the fit. Variation on top of variation, all of it controlled.

The math, when worked out, came to something like ten to a hundred trillion possible different antibody shapes. Far more than enough.

The reason no one had ever documented DNA being rearranged inside a normal cell was, of course, that no one had been looking. No one expected it. The central dogma of biology said your DNA stayed put. But your immune cells, it turned out, didn’t read the dogma. They were doing something almost no other cell in your body does: they were editing themselves, on purpose, as part of growing up. They were essentially writing their own scripts.

Tonegawa won the Nobel Prize for this in 1987. He was the only winner that year — solo Nobels are rare — and the citation called the discovery “the genetic principle for generation of antibody diversity.” Which is correct, but undersells it. What Tonegawa really proved is that part of the human genome is not fixed at all. Part of you is a deliberately scrambled deck.

The B Cell

So now we can introduce the B cell properly.

A B cell is a small, round, white blood cell that is born in your bone marrow. (The B is for bone marrow, in mammals; in chickens, where these cells were first studied, it stood for the bursa of Fabricius, an organ chickens have and you don’t. The name stuck.) Each B cell, when it is being made, runs through the V(D)J shuffle we just described. By the time it leaves the bone marrow, it is carrying its own unique antibody on its surface, the way a fisherman carries a unique hook.

Then it goes to a lymph node and waits.

For most B cells, the wait is forever. They live for a few weeks or months, never finding a match for their particular antibody, and then they die quietly and are replaced by other B cells with other random antibodies. The waste is enormous. It is also the price of the system. To have an antibody for every possible threat, you must have many, many B cells that will never meet their match.

For a small number of B cells, though, something happens. A dendritic cell — remember the messengers from the last chapter? — arrives at the lymph node carrying fragments of, say, a virus. The B cell whose surface antibody happens to fit that particular viral protein binds to it. The B cell now knows, in chemical terms, that its lottery number has come up.

What happens next is one of the most efficient processes in biology. The B cell receives a few additional signals (we’ll meet the cell that gives those signals in a moment) and begins to divide. Once a day, then twice a day, then more. Within a week there are millions of identical copies of this one cell, each carrying the same antibody, all descended from a single ancestor that happened to be carrying the right random hook on the day the virus showed up. Most of these copies become factories. They lose their old shape. They turn into round, secretion-focused cells called plasma cells, and they pour their antibodies into the bloodstream — millions of antibody molecules per cell per second.

The antibodies travel everywhere. They stick to the virus wherever they find it. They mark it for destruction by macrophages and complement. They clog up the virus’s ability to enter cells. They neutralize toxins. They clear infections.

This is the same process that happens when you get vaccinated. The vaccine is just a way of triggering the lottery without the disease. We’ll come back to that.

The T Cell (Two Kinds)

If the B cell is the system’s antibody factory, the T cell is something stranger. There are actually two main kinds of T cell, doing two different jobs, and the difference matters.

The T comes from thymus, a small pinkish-gray organ that sits behind your breastbone, just above your heart. It’s enormous when you’re a baby and shrinks as you grow up, which is funny when you learn what it does, because what the thymus does is almost the most important thing in the entire immune system. We’ll spend Chapter 7 inside the thymus. For now, just know that this is where T cells are educated, and that the education is brutal.

Like B cells, T cells use V(D)J recombination to give themselves random receptors. But T cell receptors don’t recognize whole molecules floating in the blood. T cell receptors recognize fragments of molecules being held up by other cells. This is a crucial distinction. A B cell’s antibody can grab any molecule it floats past. A T cell can only see fragments that some other cell — a dendritic cell, or a macrophage, or any infected cell — is actively presenting on its surface, like a person holding up a card.

The fragments are held up by a specific kind of molecular ID badge — the MHC molecules we mentioned in Chapter 2, which we’ll properly meet in Chapter 6. For now: think of every cell in your body holding up little cards, all the time, with samples of what’s currently inside that cell. Healthy cells hold up cards showing healthy bits of you. Infected cells hold up cards showing bits of the virus they’re infected with. T cells walk around reading the cards.

There are, broadly, two kinds of T cells reading those cards.

The first kind is called the helper T cell (or, if you like jargon, the CD4+ T cell). When a helper T cell finds a card it recognizes — held up, say, by a dendritic cell carrying viral fragments — it doesn’t kill anything. It does something more important. It releases a flood of cytokines that tell B cells to start dividing, that tell other T cells to wake up, that tell macrophages to be more aggressive, that essentially turn on the entire adaptive immune response. The helper T cell is the conductor of the orchestra. Without it, almost nothing else happens.

When HIV destroys the immune system, this is the cell it destroys. HIV specifically infects helper T cells and kills them off. After enough years, the body simply runs out of conductors, and every other immune cell — perfectly intact, perfectly competent — has no idea what to do. Infections that any healthy person could brush off become deadly. We’ll come back to this in Chapter 11.

The second kind of T cell is called the killer T cell (or, in jargon, the CD8+ T cell). It does what the name suggests. When a killer T cell finds a card it recognizes — held up, say, by one of your own cells that has been infected by a virus — it doesn’t try to disable the virus. The virus is already inside the cell, hiding, doing its damage. The killer T cell does the only thing that can solve that problem. It kills the cell.

It does this delicately, the way the NK cells in the last chapter did. A nudge, a chemical message, and the infected cell folds itself up via apoptosis. The virus inside, stripped of the cellular machinery it needs to keep replicating, dies with it. The macrophages clean up. The body trades one of its own cells to save the rest.

This is, for most viral infections, the move that ends the war. Antibodies can stop a virus from getting into cells. Once it’s in, only killer T cells can finish the job.

The Slow Burn

The first time you encounter a new virus or bacterium, the entire process we just described — dendritic cells gathering fragments, traveling to lymph nodes, finding the one B cell and the one T cell with the right random receptors, those cells dividing for a week, antibodies being produced, killer T cells being deployed — takes somewhere between five and ten days.

This is, in immunology terms, slow. It is also the reason new infections make you so sick. For the first week, your innate immune system — macrophages, neutrophils, complement, NK cells — is fighting the infection mostly alone. It is fast, but it is unsubtle. It can hold a line. It cannot win the war by itself. While it holds, you have a fever, you ache, you can’t get out of bed. The slow, precise specialists are spinning up.

Then, around day six or seven, things change. The clones have expanded. The antibodies are flowing. The killer T cells are deployed. You start to feel better, sometimes within hours.

Most viral infections — the cold, the flu, the routine illnesses of childhood — follow this exact pattern: a week of misery, a turning point, a week of recovery. The misery is your innate immune system buying time. The recovery is your adaptive immune system arriving with the specific, custom-fitted weapons it had to build from scratch.

The price of having that adaptive system is exactly that week of being sick. The reward is on the far side of it: those custom-fitted cells do not entirely go away. Some of them stay. Some of them remember. The next time you meet that virus — even years later — the response is faster, larger, and decisive. You may not even know you’ve been exposed.

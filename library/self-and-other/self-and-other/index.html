Self & Other

1. Self and Other

In 1940, a young Brazilian-British scientist named Peter Medawar was working in a hospital in Oxford when a German bomber crashed into a garden a few hundred yards from his house.

The pilot of a different plane — a British one — survived a similar crash and was brought into the burn unit. Most of his skin was gone. The doctors looked at Medawar, who was officially a zoologist but had a reputation for being the cleverest young person in any room he walked into, and asked him a question that would, more or less, define the rest of his life:

Why can’t we just take healthy skin from somebody else and put it on him?

It seemed like it should work. The pilot needed skin. There were plenty of people with skin. You could move a piece from one part of a person’s body to another — surgeons had been doing that for decades. You could give blood transfusions across people, as long as the types matched. Why not skin?

Medawar tried it. So did a lot of other people. It didn’t work.

What happened was strange and consistent. For the first few days, the borrowed skin would seem to settle in, knitting itself into the wound bed, looking pink and healthy and promising. Then, somewhere around day eight or nine, it would begin to die. Not from infection. Not from any obvious cause. It would simply darken, dry up, and slough off, as if the patient’s body had taken a few days to read the new tissue carefully, decided it was unwelcome, and personally evicted it.

Stranger still: if you tried a second graft from the same donor a few weeks later, it didn’t even get its honeymoon. It died within two or three days. The body, somehow, remembered.

Medawar spent the next decade figuring out what was going on. The answer, when it came, was so philosophically large that it eventually won him a Nobel Prize and reframed the whole question of what a body even is. In its shortest form, it is this:

Your body knows the difference between you and not-you. It is constantly checking. And once it has decided that something is not-you, it does not forget.

This sounds, when you say it out loud, almost mystical. It is not mystical. It is, in fact, one of the most carefully worked-out systems in all of biology. But to see why it’s so strange, hold the question in your head for a moment:

How does your body know what counts as you?

You are, after all, mostly made of stuff you ate. The carbon atoms in your fingernails were, six months ago, in a peach, or a chicken, or a piece of bread that was once a wheat plant in a field in Kansas. Your body has been disassembling and reassembling itself your entire life. The you-of-today contains almost no atoms in common with the you-of-ten-years-ago, except in your bones and the lenses of your eyes. And yet, somehow, your body has consistently treated all those incoming atoms as raw material, while treating a tiny fragment of someone else’s perfectly good skin as a hostile invader.

The puzzle gets worse. Your gut, right now, contains roughly thirty-eight trillion bacteria. They are not you. They have their own DNA, their own goals, their own little bacterial lives. By any reasonable definition they are not-you in a way that a piece of donor skin from your cousin really isn’t. And yet your immune system has decided to leave most of them alone — to tolerate them, to feed them, even, in some cases, to defend them from rival bacteria.

So the question isn’t really “how does the body recognize self vs. non-self.” The question is bigger and weirder:

How does the body decide what to tolerate, and what to attack, and how does it almost always get it right?

The answer is the subject of this entire book. But the very short version — the version we’ll spend the next few chapters unpacking — is that the immune system is not one thing. It is at least two systems working in concert, and probably more depending on how you count.

The first is fast. It is ancient, evolutionarily speaking — versions of it exist in jellyfish, in sea sponges, in every animal you’ve ever met. It does not learn. It does not remember. It recognizes broad categories of trouble — this protein has a pattern that looks bacterial, this surface has a pattern that looks viral — and it responds within minutes. We call it innate immunity. It is the reason a splinter gets red and warm. It is the reason a cold makes you tired and achy before you’ve even produced antibodies.

The second is slow. It takes days, sometimes weeks, to fully spin up. It is, biologically, very new — only animals with backbones have it, which is a small and recent group. But what it lacks in speed it makes up for in something almost unbelievable: it learns. It can recognize, in principle, almost any molecule that has ever existed or could ever exist, including molecules that no human has encountered in the entire history of our species. It does this by a method so strange that when biologists first figured out the mechanism, in the 1970s, a lot of older immunologists refused to believe it. We call it adaptive immunity, and it is how Medawar’s pilot’s body knew, on day eight, that the borrowed skin had to go.

Before we meet either of these systems properly, though, we need one piece of vocabulary. It is the most useful word in the whole field, and you’ll see it on every page from here on.

Antigen. (noun.) Anything the immune system can recognize and respond to. A bit of bacterial wall. A protein on the surface of a virus. A peanut, if you happen to be allergic to peanuts. A piece of someone else’s skin. A fragment of your own pancreas, if something has gone wrong. The word literally just means “thing that generates a response,” and it is gloriously generic on purpose. An antigen is whatever your immune system thinks is worth paying attention to — and the immense, complicated, occasionally tragic story of why it pays attention to some things and not others is, more or less, the story of you.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The borrowed skin looked healthy for eight days and then died, 'as if the patient's body had taken a few days to read the paperwork'. What does the delay tell you about what the body was doing?
  2. An antigen is defined as whatever the immune system thinks is worth paying attention to — deliberately generic. Why would scientists choose a word that vague?
  3. The question asked of Medawar was obvious: the pilot needs skin, people have skin, why not? When has an obvious question turned out to open a whole field?

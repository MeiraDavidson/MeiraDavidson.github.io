What Memory Does and Doesn’t Do

mRNA vaccines are the newest. Instead of giving your body a finished protein, they give your body a temporary instruction — a strand of mRNA — that tells some of your own cells to briefly make the protein themselves. Those cells then display the protein on their surface, and your immune system responds to it the way it would respond to any other foreign protein. The mRNA breaks down within hours. The cells go back to normal within days. The memory cells stay. We’ll come back to this in detail in Chapter 17, because the COVID vaccines are how most people first encountered this technology, and there’s a lot of confusion to clear up.

Most of these technologies have one thing in common: they need help getting started. The immune system is reasonably suspicious by nature, and a lone protein floating around in your shoulder muscle isn’t always alarming enough to trigger a strong response. So most vaccines include something called an adjuvant — a substance that makes the local tissue look, to your innate immune system, like there’s something genuinely dangerous going on. The adjuvant is what makes your arm sore for a day. It’s not a side effect. It’s the point. It’s the alarm bell that wakes up the dendritic cells and gets them to start gathering evidence and walking it to the lymph nodes.

The result, with all of these technologies, is the same. A few weeks after the shot, your bone marrow and your lymph nodes contain a small population of memory cells tuned to one specific dangerous thing. The cells will sit there, mostly forever, waiting. If the real pathogen ever shows up, they will be ready in hours.

Smallpox killed somewhere around three hundred million people in the twentieth century alone. The last natural case was in 1977. The disease has been eradicated from the planet. The reason is Jenner’s procedure, refined and globalized.

What Memory Does and Doesn’t Do

Two things to be honest about, before we leave this chapter.

The first is that memory isn’t perfect. Memory cells fade. Some of them die without being replaced. Some types of immunity last decades or lifetimes; others fade in years. This is why some vaccines need boosters — a tetanus shot every ten years, for instance — and others don’t. It’s also why people who had certain childhood infections or vaccines may, in older age, gradually become susceptible again. The system is excellent. It is not magic.

The second is that even perfect memory only protects you against pathogens you’ve encountered before, or things close enough to them. A wholly new pathogen — one your immune system has never seen, in a form it has never encountered — starts the slow process from scratch, the same way it always has. This is part of why pandemics of new viruses can spread so widely so fast: not because anyone’s immune system is broken, but because no one’s immune system has the relevant memory yet. The first encounter takes about a week. The second is faster. But the first is the first.

Vaccines are, more than anything else, a way of making the first encounter survivable.

There’s one more thing about memory worth mentioning before we move on, because it’s strange and recent and not yet fully understood.

For a long time, immunologists assumed that immune memory — the long-term, learning, decades-spanning kind — was something only B cells and T cells could do. That was the rule. The innate cells, the macrophages and neutrophils and NK cells we met in Chapter 2, did not learn. They did not remember. They did the same thing every time.

In the last fifteen years or so, that picture has started to change. Researchers have begun to find evidence that some innate cells — macrophages, NK cells — do something that looks suspiciously like remembering. Not in the precise, antigen-specific way of B cells and T cells. More like a general tuning. After a strong infection, these cells appear to remain in a slightly more reactive state, more alert, more ready to respond, for weeks or months afterward. The phenomenon has a name now — trained immunity — and it’s one of the more active areas of immunology research right now.

This is a useful thing to know about, partly because you’ll see it in news articles, and partly because it’s a good example of something this book wants to keep emphasizing: immunology is not finished. Big, basic ideas are still being worked out. The textbook of 2050 will look different from the textbook of 2025, and not because the textbook of 2025 was wrong. It was just incomplete, the way every working description of a living system always is.

What we know for sure is that your body, right now, contains a library. Not a metaphorical library — a physical one, distributed across your bone marrow and your lymph nodes and your spleen, made of millions of cells, each one carrying the molecular signature of something your body once met and survived. Childhood illnesses. Vaccines. Infections you don’t even remember having. Maybe a stomach bug from a vacation in 2008. Each of them is in there, written down in cellular form, ready to be read again if it has to be.

The next time you go to a doctor and they look at your chart and ask whether you’ve ever had measles, or whether you got your tetanus booster, what they are really asking is: what’s in your library?

Most of what’s in your library, you have no idea about. Most of it, you’ll never need. But it’s there. It is one of the quieter, weirder forms of identity. You are not just what you eat or what you’ve done. You are also what your immune system remembers.

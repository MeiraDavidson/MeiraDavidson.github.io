A Fever Is a Feature

Lymph nodes are something like the city’s town halls. They are small, bean-shaped structures dotted throughout your body — under your jaw, in your armpits, in your groin, all along the inside of your torso. (When a doctor feels around your neck while you have a cold and says “your glands are up,” they mean your lymph nodes are swollen, because they are full of cells doing exactly what we’re about to describe.) Inside each lymph node, millions of T cells and B cells are waiting — specialists, each one tuned to recognize one specific molecular pattern.

The dendritic cell arrives at the lymph node holding its evidence and, in effect, walks down the line: does anyone recognize this? Anyone? Most don’t. But sooner or later, one specialist — out of millions — does. And when that happens, the slow, learning, terrifyingly precise second half of the immune system comes online.

That’s the next chapter.

A Fever Is a Feature

Before we leave the first responders, one more thing.

When you are sick — when you have the flu, or a serious infection, or even just a bad cold — your body does something that, viewed from the outside, looks like a malfunction. It raises its own temperature. You become hot to the touch. You shiver under blankets. Your appetite collapses. You feel awful.

For most of human history, people assumed fever was the disease. It looked like the disease. It made you feel like the disease. The natural thing to do, if you were a doctor in 1750 or even 1950, was to bring the fever down. Your patient would feel better, and that seemed like progress.

We now know — and this took longer to figure out than you might think — that fever is not the disease. Fever is your body’s response to the disease. And that response is, in most cases, helpful.

When your immune cells release certain cytokines, those cytokines travel to your brain, specifically to a small region called the hypothalamus, which is your body’s thermostat. The cytokines turn the thermostat up. The hypothalamus then orders the rest of your body to produce more heat — by shivering, by burning more energy — and to lose less of it, by constricting blood vessels near the skin (which is why you feel cold even though you’re hot).

The result is a body running several degrees above normal. And here is what is happening in that hotter body. Many bacteria and viruses replicate poorly above 37 degrees Celsius. A few degrees of fever can dramatically slow them down. Meanwhile, your immune cells — the ones we’ve been meeting in this chapter — work better at higher temperatures. They move faster. They communicate faster. They divide more readily. Your body, in essence, is making the playing field bad for the invaders and good for the defenders.

This is also why you lose your appetite when you’re sick, and why you sleep so much. Both responses are coordinated by the same cytokine signals. Sleep gives the immune system the resources it needs to fight. Reduced appetite — counterintuitive though it sounds — appears to starve certain pathogens that depend on certain nutrients in your gut. Not eating much when you have a stomach bug is, biologically, what your body wants you to do.

None of this is to say you should never take a fever-reducing medicine. A very high fever, especially in a small child, can be dangerous, and there are good reasons to manage it. But it’s worth understanding what fever is. It is not your body breaking. It is your body working — uncomfortably, expensively, but on purpose.

The same is true, in different forms, for almost every symptom of being sick. The runny nose. The cough. The aches. The misery. None of those are the infection itself. They are your immune system doing its job. The cost of the defense is paid by you — in fatigue, discomfort, missed school days — but the defense is the point.

So this is the front line. Macrophages on patrol, eating what doesn’t belong. Neutrophils sprinting in by the billion when summoned, doing damage as fast as they can. NK cells walking the streets looking for cells that have stopped wearing their badges. Complement, drifting in the bloodstream, latching onto bacteria the way frost latches onto a window. Dendritic cells gathering evidence and walking it to the town hall. And cytokines — chemical shouts in the air — coordinating all of it.

It is fast. It is old. It is genuinely incredible.

But it has a limit. It can recognize categories. It cannot recognize specifics. A macrophage knows that a certain pattern of molecules is “bacterial.” It does not know which bacterium. A complement protein can tell that a surface “isn’t ours.” It cannot tell which one.

For most threats, that’s fine. But for the ones that learn — the viruses that mutate, the bacteria that evolve — the body needs cells that can learn back. Cells that can recognize one specific shape, out of all possible shapes. Cells that can remember. Cells that can do, in chemistry, what Medawar’s pilot’s body did when it rejected its second skin graft within three days.

Those cells exist. They live in the lymph nodes. The dendritic cells are walking toward them right now, holding up their evidence files, asking the question that started this book:

Does anyone here recognize this?

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Metchnikoff stayed home from the circus, jabbed a starfish larva with a tangerine thorn, and watched. How much of science looks like that, and how much looks like the version in textbooks?
  2. He paced his apartment, unable to sit still, because he understood he was watching a body defend itself. What would it feel like to be the only person alive who knows something?
  3. Innate defences handle anything that does not learn. For threats that evolve, the body needs cells that can learn back. Why does an enemy that adapts require a different kind of defence entirely?

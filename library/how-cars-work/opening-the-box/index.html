How Cars Work

Opening the Box

PROLOGUE

Look at a toy car for a minute. The kind that fits in one hand. You can see the wheels turning when you push it across the floor. You can flip it over and see how the wheels are mounted on a thin metal axle. Push it harder and it goes farther; push it on carpet and it barely goes at all. Nothing is hidden. Toy cars make sense.

Now think about a real car.

You climb into the back seat. The door slams. Your mom turns a key or pushes a button. The car makes a sound — maybe a quiet hum, maybe a deeper rumble — and then it moves. It carries you and your mom and your backpack and a week of groceries down the street. It does this for an hour, or for fifty miles, or for as long as anyone wants. Nobody is pushing it. There is no obvious reason it should be moving.

Real cars do not make sense the way toy cars do. They are sealed boxes. The wheels turn, and the car goes, but you cannot see why.

This book is about why.

We are going to open the box. Not literally — your mom will not be thrilled if you start unscrewing the hood — but in your head. By the end of the book, when you hear a car start, you will know what is actually happening inside it. When the brake lights come on at a stop sign, you will know what is grabbing the wheels. When you pass an electric car at a charging station, you will know what is flowing into its battery and what that battery is actually doing.

Three parts

The first part is about old cars — cars from your grandparents’ time, the kind made of metal and rubber and not much else. These cars are mostly mechanical. That means almost everything that happens, you could see happening if you took the car apart. Pieces push other pieces. Gears turn other gears. There is a logic to it that you can follow with your eyes.

The second part is about how cars got smart. Sometime around the 1980s, people started putting computers in cars. At first just little ones, doing small jobs. Now there are dozens of computers in a regular car, talking to each other, watching the road, deciding things in fractions of a second. Modern cars are still mechanical underneath, but the mechanical parts are managed by software.

The third part is about electric cars. These are different. No engine, no gasoline, no exhaust pipe. A giant battery and an electric motor and not much else. Simpler in some ways, stranger in others.

How to read it

Each chapter has a Try It — something you can actually do, usually with stuff already in the kitchen. A candle and a jar. A balloon. A magnet. A bike. These are not extras. They are the point. Reading about how something works will only get you so far. Doing the small version yourself is what makes it stick.

There are also blue boxes scattered through the chapters with surprising stories or odd facts about the people and machines involved. Read them or skip them. They are not on the test, because there is no test.

You don’t drive yet. That’s fine. You don’t need to drive to understand a car, the same way you don’t need to fly a plane to understand how a wing works. You just need to pay attention.

Let’s open the box.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The author says a toy car “makes sense” in a way a real car does not. What is it about being able to see the parts that makes something feel understandable? Name something else you own that is a sealed box.
  2. Every chapter has a Try It, and the author insists these “are not extras, they are the point.” Do you agree that doing a small version yourself teaches you more than reading does? Can you think of something you only really understood after trying it?
  3. The book is organised by era — the 1960s car, the 1990s car, the electric car — rather than by part. Why might that order work better for a reader than starting with the engine and working outward?

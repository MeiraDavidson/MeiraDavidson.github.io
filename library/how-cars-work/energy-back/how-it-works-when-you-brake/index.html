How it works when you brake

Picture an electric car driving down the road at 30 miles per hour. The motor controller is feeding current to the stator coils, the rotor is spinning, and through one fixed gear ratio, the wheels are spinning along with it.

Your mom takes her foot off the pedal.

The motor controller stops feeding current to the coils. But the car doesn’t stop instantly — it has all that kinetic energy, the wheels are still rolling, the rotor is still spinning. From the wheels, the motion is being fed back through the gear ratio to the rotor, just as it always was.

So now the rotor is spinning, the stator coils are no longer being fed current, and Chapter 3’s rule is still in effect. The rotor’s magnets are sweeping past the coils, magnetic fields are changing, and voltages are appearing in the coils.

The motor controller has a choice. It can leave the coils disconnected, in which case the voltages just sit there and the car coasts (slowing only from air drag and rolling friction). Or it can connect the coils to the battery.

If it connects them, the induced voltages drive current INTO the battery. The battery starts to charge. And here is the critical part: pulling current out of the coils makes the rotor harder to spin. The same physics that gives you a generator effect also creates a drag on the rotation.

The car slows down. The battery gains a little energy. The kinetic energy that would have become wasted heat in the brake pads is captured back.

That is regenerative braking. The car’s kinetic energy travels through the wheels, through the gear ratio, through the rotor, through the stator coils, through the controller, and into the battery. The reverse of the trip it took on the way out.

One-pedal driving

In a gasoline car, the driver uses two pedals to control speed. The right one (gas) makes the car go faster. The left one (brake) makes it go slower. Lifting off the gas just lets the car coast — slowing very gradually from air drag, but not really braking.

In a modern EV, the accelerator pedal does both jobs.

Press it down: the motor pulls power from the battery. The harder you press, the more current flows, the more force the motor makes, the harder the car accelerates.

Lift it most of the way off: the motor controller switches the system into generator mode. The further you lift (the closer to fully off), the more current flows back into the battery, and the more drag the motor produces on the wheels. The car slows down — sometimes hard enough to come to a near-stop without anyone ever touching the actual brake pedal.

This is called one-pedal driving, and many EV drivers swear by it. After a few weeks of it, drivers stop using the brake pedal except for emergencies and the last mile per hour or two of stopping. The accelerator does both adding and subtracting energy, with no transition.

It also makes the car much smoother to drive. There is no jump between gas-pedal physics and brake-pedal physics. It is all one continuous control. Press a little harder, accelerate a little harder. Lift a little, decelerate a little. How far the pedal is from fully pressed directly determines how energy is moving between the wheels and the battery — in either direction.

How Cars Work · Energy Back

What regen can’t do

Regenerative braking is not magic. It has real limits.

It can’t capture 100% of the energy. Some of the kinetic energy is always lost — as heat in the motor windings, as heat in the wires, as friction in the bearings. Real EVs typically recover somewhere between 50% and 70% of the energy of any given stop. The rest still becomes heat, just less of it than in a gas car.

It can’t always run. If the battery is already fully charged, there is nowhere to put the captured energy, and regen has to be reduced or shut off. The car falls back on its friction brakes. The same is true if the battery is too cold to accept fast charging. EV computers watch this constantly and switch smoothly between regen and friction braking based on what the battery can do at that moment.

It can’t bring the car to a full stop on its own. As the rotor slows, the rate of magnetic field change drops, and the induced voltage drops with it. Below a few miles per hour, regen has almost no slowing effect. The friction brakes — the same disc-and-pad ones from Chapter 5 — have to handle the last bit of every stop.

It can’t help in an emergency. If a driver slams on the pedal because something is in the road, the car needs maximum stopping force right now, and that means friction brakes do almost all the work. Every EV still has a complete friction brake system at all four wheels for exactly this reason.

What regen does very well is the everyday case: gentle slowing, stopping at red lights, coasting down hills, easing through traffic. In those situations it captures most of the energy that would otherwise be lost. Across a full day of normal driving, regen typically extends an EV’s range by ten to twenty percent — with the bigger gains coming on the slow, stop-and-go streets where braking happens often, and almost no gain on the highway, where the car spends most of its time cruising and rarely brakes at all.

Try It: Spin a Motor Backwards

This Try It works best if you still have the motor you built in Chapter 10. (If you don’t, any small DC motor — the kind inside many electric toys — will work.) You will also need a multimeter.

Set the multimeter to read DC volts on its lowest range (often labeled “200 mV” or similar). Touch one probe to each lead of the motor. The reading should be at or near zero. Now spin the motor’s shaft as fast as you can with your fingers — pinch it between thumb and forefinger and twist.

Watch the multimeter. The number jumps. It might only be a few millivolts (thousandths of a volt), but it is real, and it is coming from your spinning. The faster you spin, the higher the voltage.

You have just turned the motor into a generator, by hand.

Now connect the motor’s leads to a small battery — like the AA battery you used in the original build — and watch what happens. The motor spins on its own. (Same device, opposite direction.)

If you happen to have a small low-voltage LED, you can try the more dramatic version: connect the LED across the motor’s leads (LEDs only light when current flows in one direction, so if it doesn’t light, swap the leads). Spin the shaft fast. The LED flickers. You are powering an LED with the motion of your fingers, through a generator that costs about $2 to build.

Scale this up — bigger motor, stronger magnets, the wheels of a car instead of your fingers — and you have an EV’s regenerative braking system.

Did You Know? Subway trains have been doing regenerative braking for over a hundred years. Electric subway trains run on power supplied by an overhead wire or a third rail. When a train brakes, its motors switch to generator mode (the same trick we just covered), and the recovered electricity flows BACK into the rail. Another train somewhere on the network — usually one that is currently accelerating — can pull that electricity right out of the rail and use it. Twenty to forty percent of a modern subway’s electricity comes from the regenerative braking of other trains on the same line. If you ride the New York City subway, the London Underground, or any major metro system, you are riding inside a giant regenerative braking network. Every time the train you are on slows down, you might be helping power another train, miles away, that is just leaving its own station.

That is the electric car

Battery in the floor. Motor at each driven axle. Controller deciding, second by second, when to push current out (to drive) and when to pull it back (to brake). A few hundred pounds of lithium ions, a few coils of copper wire, a few permanent magnets, and the math of changing magnetic fields. From those ingredients, a car.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Chapter 5 said there was nowhere else for a car’s energy to go but heat. This chapter says there is. Was the earlier chapter wrong, or was it answering a narrower question?
  2. A motor and a generator are the same device run in opposite directions. What other machine can you think of that does its job backwards as well as forwards?
  3. On a busy metro line, twenty to forty per cent of the electricity comes from other trains braking. Why does a network of trains recover so much more than a single car can?

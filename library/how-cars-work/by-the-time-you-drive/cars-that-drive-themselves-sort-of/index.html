Cars that drive themselves (sort of)

The big question for the next decade is how much of the driving the car will do for you.

The honest answer in 2026 is: more than ten years ago, less than the headlines suggest.

A typical new car can already do many things on its own. It can keep itself in its lane on a highway. It can match the speed of the car in front and brake to a stop in traffic. It can park itself in a tight spot. It can warn the driver about cars in the blind spot, pedestrians stepping into the road, and obstacles up ahead. (Most of these were the safety systems from Chapter 7.)

What it generally cannot do, in 2026, is drive entirely without you watching. Every car sold for personal use in 2026 expects a human driver to be paying attention at all times, ready to take over. The systems are very good helpers. They are not yet pilots.

Self-driving taxis, on the other hand, do exist. In a few cities — Phoenix, San Francisco, parts of Los Angeles — companies operate cars without drivers, picking people up the way Uber drivers do. These cars have spinning sensors on their roofs and a dozen cameras around their bodies. They drive better than most people in some ways (they never get distracted) and worse in others (they sometimes get confused by construction zones, snow, or people doing weird things in the road). They run only in cities they have been carefully mapped for. They are not yet everywhere.

Whether self-driving cars become normal in your lifetime depends on hard problems that haven’t all been solved. A computer driving in 99% of situations is doable. A computer driving in 100% of situations — every weird corner case, every unusual weather condition, every time someone does something the software didn’t predict — is much, much harder. The difference between 99 and 100 is enormous.

By the time you drive, your car will probably be able to do more for you than your mom’s car can today. Whether you will ever own a car that drives itself completely is genuinely uncertain.

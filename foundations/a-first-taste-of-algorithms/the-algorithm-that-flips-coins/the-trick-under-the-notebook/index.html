The trick under the notebook

One debt from Chapter 4 remains unpaid. The notebook — Python’s dictionary — answers “is this question already written down?” essentially instantly, on notebooks of any size. Lists can’t do that (finding a thing in an unsorted list is Chapter 1’s linear search). What is the dictionary doing?

It is gambling, and winning the same way quicksort does. The scheme is called hashing: keep k buckets; put each key through a fixed scrambling function — the hash function — that turns the key into a bucket number, spreading keys across buckets like a fair die; to look a key up, recompute its bucket number and look only there. One arithmetic step to find the bucket, and then a search within it — fast exactly when buckets stay small.

So everything turns on collisions: different keys landing in the same bucket. How soon do collisions appear? That question has a famous, disguised twin. A class of k students, 365 possible birthdays: how likely is it that some two students share one? Intuition says you’d need a hundred-plus students for an even chance. The truth — compute it, then simulate it in the lab until belief arrives — is 23. With 23 people, the probability of a shared birthday already exceeds one half (0.507, exactly); at 50 people it is 0.97. The reason intuition fails by a factor of five: you instinctively count people, but collisions are about pairs, and 23 people contain 253 pairs — each pair an unlikely coincidence, but 253 lottery tickets add up. (The Diamond has you prove the threshold of 23 with one inequality from Book 2 and no calculator, via a numerical coincidence so clean it feels illegal.)

Figure 7.2 — The birthday curve: probability of a shared birthday against group size, crossing one-half at 23 and saturating by 50.

The birthday arithmetic delivers hashing’s honest verdict, both halves. The pessimistic half: collisions arrive shockingly early — around √k keys into k buckets — so no hash table outruns collisions, and the design must survive them (each bucket holds a little list; lookups search it). The optimistic half: early collisions are not many collisions. Keep roughly as many buckets as keys, and the average bucket holds about one item — so the average lookup is an arithmetic step plus a glance: constant time, on average, by the same logic that priced the hats. Chapter 4’s miracle, retroactively explained: the notebook was fast because it was gambling on a fair scramble, and the gamble concentrates just like quicksort’s. The two halves of this chapter are one idea wearing two costumes — randomness, honestly priced, converts worst-case dread into average-case contracts you can measure, trust, and build on.

Deeper water: the average, exactly

Quicksort’s expected comparisons, by linearity. Sort distinct items; name them by rank, 1 through n. Decompose: for each pair i < j, let the pair’s contribution be 1 if i and j are ever compared, else 0; the total comparisons is the sum over all pairs.

When are ranks i and j compared? Only when one of them is chosen as a pivot while both are still in the same sublist — and the sublist containing both always contains the whole stretch i, i+1, …, j (nothing between them can leave for the other side before they’re separated). Watch that stretch of j − i + 1 items: the first of them to be picked as a pivot decides everything. If it’s i or j, they are compared (the pivot meets everyone present); if it’s anything strictly between, they are sent to opposite sides, never to meet. By the fairness of the coins, each of the j − i + 1 candidates is equally likely to be first, so

P(i, j compared) = 2 / (j − i + 1).

Adjacent ranks are always compared (2/2 = 1 — someone must separate them, and only they themselves can); the extreme pair almost never (2/n). Sum over all pairs, group by distance d = j − i, and the sum telescopes into harmonic numbers: E = 2(n+1)Hₙ − 4n, where Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … + 1/n ≈ ln n. Hence E ≈ 2n ln n for large n — with the honest small-print this book owes you: at n = 256 the exact formula gives ≈ 2,125 while the asymptotic slogan says 2,839, a third too high. Your histogram’s hill will sit on the exact number, not the slogan. Asymptotics are shadows; at toy sizes, measure.

Deeper water: two kinds of gambler

Quicksort’s gamble never risks the answer — every run sorts correctly; only the time is random. Algorithms of this temperament are called Las Vegas. The other temperament — fixed time, but a small probability of a wrong answer — is called Monte Carlo, after the vignette’s method, and it is not a compromise for the desperate: the fastest known ways to test whether a 600-digit number is prime are Monte Carlo, wrong with probability less than one in 2¹⁰⁰ per run — and runs can be repeated, squaring the doubt away, until the error probability is dwarfed by the chance of the computer being struck by a meteor mid-calculation. At that point, philosophy must decide what “knowing” ever meant; engineering, sensibly, ships it. (Your phone performed such a test, several times, the last time it opened a secure connection.)

Where this leaves us

Predictability was the vulnerability: any fixed rule, read by an adversary, has a killer input. Coins removed the vulnerability by removing the readability — bad inputs became bad luck, and bad luck, priced by linearity and verified by histogram, turned out to be a tight hill far from the cliff. The same gamble runs the dictionary: scramble fairly, collide early but thinly, answer in constant time on average. And “average” itself was promoted from a vague comfort to a computed, concentrated, instrument-checked quantity.

Hold that promotion carefully, because the book’s second act now begins, and it runs on nothing else. Chapter 8 asks what it means for a machine to learn — and the answer, it will turn out, is built from this chapter’s materials: a quantity averaged over examples, minimized by a greedy walk, trusted not because any single answer is guaranteed but because the average is honest and the distribution concentrates. “Approximately correct, on average” sounds like a retreat from Chapter 1’s two questions. It is actually their final form.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. An adversary can choose the worst input for any fixed rule. How does flipping a coin take that power away?
  2. 'Fast not always, not in the worst case, but on average, in a sense that can be made precise, trusted, and measured.' Which of those four words is doing the most work?
  3. Would you fly in a plane whose software was fast on average? What makes that question different for sorting than for flight control?

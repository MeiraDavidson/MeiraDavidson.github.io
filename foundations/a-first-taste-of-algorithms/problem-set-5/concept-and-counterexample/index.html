A First Taste of Algorithms · Problem Set 5

Concept & Counterexample

3.1 Claim: “DFS, like BFS, finds shortest paths — it explores systematically, after all.” Build the smallest maze or graph where DFS from S (neighbors explored in alphabetical order) reaches T by a path strictly longer than the shortest, and state in one sentence what the thread does that the ripple never does.

3.2 Claim: “BFS handles weighted graphs: just treat the weights as distances.” Three vertices suffice for the refutation — exhibit weights where the fewest-hops route is not the cheapest route, and say which ring BFS puts the destination in versus what Dijkstra’s tags say.

3.3 Claim: “Dijkstra works fine with a few negative weights as long as the total stays positive.” Build the three-vertex counterexample the chapter promised: Home→B direct weight 2, Home→C weight 3, C→B weight −2. Trace the protocol — at what moment does it settle a lie? Point to the sentence in the deep-water proof that your example kills, quoting its load-bearing clause.

3.4 Claim: “adding an edge to a graph never makes any shortest path longer.” True or false for nonnegative weights? Prove or refute. Then the sneaky cousin: “adding an edge never makes any DFS route longer.” (Careful: what does DFS’s route even depend on?)

3.5 Claim: “if a graph is connected, BFS from any vertex visits all vertices; therefore, to check connectivity, you must run BFS from every vertex.” Keep the first half, demolish the second: how many BFS runs does connectivity-checking actually need, and why?

Diamond

4.1 The rings are the distances. Prove: when BFS runs from vertex s, every vertex v it visits is assigned to ring d(v), where d(v) is the true minimum number of edges from s to v — and vertices never visited have no path from s at all. Structure it as induction on rings: (a) Floor: ring 0. (b) Step, two halves that students always conflate, so keep them apart: (i) everything BFS puts in ring k + 1 truly has distance ≤ k + 1 (exhibit the path — where does it come from?); (ii) nothing of true distance k + 1 gets missed or placed later (its neighbor on a shortest path lives in ring k by hypothesis — finish the sentence, and find the precise line of the queue’s discipline that this half leans on). (c) The absence clause: prove the never-visited claim by contradiction, walking a hypothetical path from s and locating its first never-visited vertex. (d) One sentence: which half of (b) fails for DFS, and which for BFS-with-weights? (Your counterexamples from 3.1 and 3.2 are the witnesses; the proof tells you where they strike.)

Computational Lab

5.1 Lab 5 in full, predictions before runs. Your hand-run of Dijkstra (Fluency 1.5) must be checked against the machine’s tags before you trust either.

Teach It

6.1 Tell someone the Königsberg story with a napkin: draw the city, then perform Euler’s deletion — redraw as four dots and seven lines while they watch — and let them hunt for the walk before you show the parity argument. Three sentences: did the abstraction step or the parity step impress them more, and why do you think that is?

6.2 Run the Dijkstra coin protocol with someone on a map of your actual neighborhood (six or seven places, walking-minutes as weights, estimated honestly). Let them be the bureaucrat — they move the coins, you only enforce the two rules. Report the first moment a detour beat a direct edge on your streets, and whether they trusted it.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Which of the three ways of walking a graph would you use to find the shortest route, and why not the other two?
  2. Draw a graph where your intuition gives the wrong answer. What makes it misleading?
  3. The chapter says three ways of walking answer almost everything. What does 'almost' leave out?

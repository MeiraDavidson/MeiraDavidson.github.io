A First Taste of Algorithms · Problem Set 2

Concept & Counterexample

3.1 Claim: “for scheduling, longest-first can’t be optimal, but it’s a decent rule.” Refute it with the smallest counterexample you can build, and explain in one sentence what longest-first ignores. (You are re-running the chapter’s hunt with a new wrong rule.)

3.2 Claim: “earliest-finish-first works only because the events have different lengths; if all events were exactly one hour long, earliest-start-first would tie it.” Decide, then prove or refute. (If you believe it, find the exchange argument; if not, the counterexample needs care precisely because all bars are equal length.)

3.3 Fewest-conflicts, refuted. The rule “attend the event that conflicts with the fewest others, then repeat” feels wise. Verify it fails on this exact instance — events (start, finish): chain (1,3), (3,5), (5,7), (7,9); blockers (2,4), (2,4), (2,4), (6,8), (6,8), (6,8). Count each event’s conflicts, run fewest-conflicts-first, run earliest-finish-first, and report both scores. Then say in one sentence why “fewest conflicts” was the wrong measure of trouble.

3.4 Claim: “greedy change fails on {1, 7, 10} because 7 doesn’t divide 10.” Test the divisibility theory: find a coin system where no coin divides the next yet greedy still succeeds on every amount (audit it!), or a system with clean divisibility that fails. Then state carefully what divisibility actually guarantees — show that when each coin divides the next ({1, 5, 25}, say), greedy is optimal, and find where your argument uses the divisibility.

3.5 The finite certificate. Run your audit loop on US coins {1, 5, 10, 25} for every amount from 1 to 34. Combined with the Kozen–Zaks theorem (deep water; quote it, don’t prove it), write a five-sentence proof that American greedy change is optimal for every amount, forever. Be precise about what the computation established and what the theorem added — neither alone suffices.

Diamond

4.1 Greedy stays ahead, completed. Starting from the deep-water lemma finish(gᵢ) ≤ finish(sᵢ): (a) Prove the lemma’s base case and inductive step in your own words, drawing the timeline picture that makes the step obvious. (b) Derive the conclusion: greedy’s schedule is at least as large as any valid schedule. (Suppose the rival has m > k events; apply the lemma at position k and contradict greedy’s stopping rule — what exactly does greedy’s having stopped after g_k assert about the events that remain?) (c) The boundary audit: our convention says finishing at 1:00 and starting at 1:00 don’t conflict. Locate every place in (a) and (b) where ≤ versus < matters, and state whether the theorem survives if back-to-back events are counted as conflicts. (d) One sentence: where would this proof break if you tried to run it on the {1, 7, 10} coins instead of on intervals?

Computational Lab

5.1 Lab 2 in full, predictions written before runs. The {1,7,10} failure pattern you conjecture in Break-it 2 should be stated as a precise sentence and tested on the range 100–130 before running it.

Teach It

6.1 Give someone the overbooked-Saturday table and ask them to attend as many things as possible, watching what rule they use. They will almost certainly reach for “earliest start” or “the ones I like most.” Let them commit to an answer, then reveal earliest-finish-first and have them check it beats their rule. Three sentences: what was their first instinct, and what convinced them it was wrong?

6.2 Teach the {1,7,10} disaster with actual objects — coins, beans, Lego of two sizes. Rules: they must make the greedy mistake themselves first (set up the 14 so their hands grab the 10), and you may not say “greedy” or “algorithm” until after the 7 + 7 reveal. Write three sentences: what did their face do, and what explanation finally landed?

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Problem 6.2 asks you to make someone grab the 10 first and discover 7 + 7 themselves. Why insist they make the mistake before you name it?
  2. Greedy never loses with ordinary coins but loses badly in a country with 1, 7 and 10. What is special about the coin sets where it works?
  3. Design your own coin system where greedy fails worse than the book's example. What did you have to do to break it?

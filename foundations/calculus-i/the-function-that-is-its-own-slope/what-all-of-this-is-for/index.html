Calculus I · The Function That Is Its Own Slope

What all of this is for

Time to collect on the three real-world situations that started the chapter. They were all the same equation, dNdt=kN\dfrac{dN}{dt} = kN — “the rate of change is proportional to the amount.” We can now write down its solution and check it ourselves:

N(t)=N0ekt.N(t) = N_{0}\,e^{kt}.

Here N0N_0 is the amount at the start (set t=0t=0: e0=1e^0 = 1, so N(0)=N0N(0) = N_0 — good). Does it actually solve the equation? Differentiate, using the chain rule:

dNdt=N0⋅kekt=k(N0ekt)=kN.\frac{dN}{dt} = N_{0}\cdot k\,e^{kt} = k\,(N_{0}e^{kt}) = k\,N.

Yes. The derivative came back equal to kk times the function itself, which is exactly the equation we needed to satisfy. We have not just been handed the answer — we have verified it.

Growth: money and rabbits

When k>0k > 0, N(t)=N0ektN(t) = N_0 e^{kt} grows, and it grows faster the bigger it gets. This is continuous compounding: a bank account growing at rate rr, compounded not yearly or monthly but every instant, reaches N0ertN_0 e^{rt} after time tt. (This is also where (1+1n)n\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n secretly lives — compounding nn times a year and letting n→∞n \to \infty is what produces ee in the first place. The promise from earlier, peeking through again.)

Decay and half-life: atoms and medicine

When k<0k < 0, write it as k=−λk = -\lambda with λ>0\lambda > 0, and the same function now decays:

N(t)=N0e−λt.N(t) = N_{0}\,e^{-\lambda t}.

This describes a radioactive sample losing atoms, and — the example to keep in your pocket if you care about drug design — a medicine being cleared from the bloodstream. Most drugs leave the body by first-order kinetics: the liver and kidneys remove an amount proportional to how much is currently circulating, which is exactly dNdt=−λN\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N.

The number everyone quotes for such a process is its half-life: the time t1/2t_{1/2} for the amount to fall to half of whatever it was. We can derive it instead of memorizing it. We want the time when NN has dropped to N0/2N_0/2:

N0e−λt1/2=12N0.N_{0}\,e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \tfrac{1}{2}N_{0}.

Cancel N0N_0, then take ln\ln of both sides to pull the exponent down (this is the moment logarithms were built for — undoing an exponential):

e−λt1/2=12⇒−λt1/2=ln⁡12=−ln⁡2⇒t1/2=ln⁡2λ.e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; -\lambda t_{1/2} = \ln\tfrac12 = -\ln 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\,t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\,}.

Worked example. A painkiller has an elimination rate constant λ=0.231\lambda = 0.231 per hour. What is its half-life, and how much of a 400 mg400\text{ mg} dose remains after 99 hours?

Half-life: t1/2=ln⁡20.231=0.6930.231=3.0t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.231} = \dfrac{0.693}{0.231} = 3.0 hours. So the amount halves every 33 hours.

After 99 hours: that is exactly three half-lives, so we expect three halvings: 400→200→100→50 mg400 \to 200 \to 100 \to 50\text{ mg}. Check it the precise way too: N(9)=400e−0.231×9=400e−2.079=400×0.125=50 mgN(9) = 400\,e^{-0.231\times 9} = 400\,e^{-2.079} = 400 \times 0.125 = 50\text{ mg}. The two methods agree, which is how you know you did it right. (Figure 5.4: the decay curve with the dose halving at t=3,6,9t = 3, 6, 9 hours marked.)

Why log scales exist

Because ln\ln turns multiplication into addition, it squashes enormous multiplicative ranges into walkable additive ones. That is why the loud, scientific scales of the world are logarithmic: earthquake energy (Richter), sound (decibels), acidity (pH). A jump of “one unit” on these scales means times ten, not plus ten. When someone tells you a magnitude-77 quake is “a bit worse” than a magnitude-55, the logarithm is the reason they are off by a factor of a hundred.

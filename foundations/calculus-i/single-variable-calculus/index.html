The Mathematics Foundation

The Curious Family Library is a family of plain-spoken, build-it-yourself guides across many subjects. This book continues its Mathematics line — Module Two of a small, deliberately ordered foundation. Its aim is easy to say and ambitious to do: to give you the backbone of mathematics — quickly, and deeply enough that it lasts — so you can dive into whatever you choose: artificial intelligence, engineering, medicine, science, or a field that doesn’t even have a name yet.

The goal is never to memorize. It is to understand well enough to rebuild. Every idea here is earned from a reason, so you walk away with not a list of facts but two durable habits: the critical thinking to ask whether a claim is really true, and the power to derive what you need when you’ve forgotten the formula — which, in real life, is always. Master the foundation and you needn’t carry the whole library in your head; you can regenerate it.

The modules of the foundation:

  1. Numbers and Functions — the number systems, and the handful of function families that everything else is built from.
  2. Calculus I (this book) — single variable: limits, derivatives, integrals — the mathematics of change and accumulation.
  3. Linear Algebra — vectors, matrices, and the language of high dimensions. It sits here on purpose: Calculus II leans on it (and it is the language modern AI lives in).
  4. Calculus II — several variables: the ideas of Calculus I set loose in more than one dimension, standing on the vectors and matrices just learned.
  5. Probability and Statistics — reasoning carefully about uncertainty, data, and evidence.
  6. The Summary — a short capstone that ties the whole foundation together and shows how the pieces connect.

They’re meant to be read roughly in order — each leans on the ones before — but every module stands on its own feet. Computing and algorithms have a line of their own in the Library; this is the backbone they rest on.

Every chapter of this book has an interactive companion notebook — sliders, animations, and drills — and all of its code and figures are open. They live at https://github.com/MeiraDavidson/math_module2, in the notebooks/ folder.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The stated goal is never to memorise but to understand well enough to rebuild — so that you can regenerate a formula you have forgotten. Has any subject ever been taught to you that way? What would change if more were?
  2. The two habits promised are asking whether a claim is really true, and deriving what you need when you have forgotten the formula. Which of those two do you think school actually trains?
  3. The modules are meant to be read roughly in order, each leaning on the ones before, but every one stands on its own. What is the tension in designing something that way?

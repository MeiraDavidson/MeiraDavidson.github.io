Calculus I · Adding Up Forever

Principle 1 — Comparison: trap it between sums you know

The first principle is exactly what you’d do by instinct: if your series is term-by-term smaller than one you know converges, it must converge too; if it’s larger than one you know diverges, it must diverge.

Comparison. Suppose 0≤an≤bn0 \le a_n \le b_n for all nn. If ∑bn\displaystyle\sum b_n converges, then ∑an\displaystyle\sum a_n converges. If ∑an\displaystyle\sum a_n diverges, then ∑bn\displaystyle\sum b_n diverges.

The geometric series is usually the thing you compare against. For instance, ∑12n+1\displaystyle\sum \frac{1}{2^n + 1} has every term smaller than 12n\tfrac{1}{2^n}, and ∑12n\sum \tfrac{1}{2^n} is a convergent geometric series — so by comparison, ∑12n+1\sum \tfrac{1}{2^n+1} converges. The whole skill is recognizing what to compare to: bound your complicated series above by a simple convergent one (to prove convergence), or below by a simple divergent one (to prove divergence). Principle 2 — The integral test: weigh the sum against an area

The second principle is the one that earns this chapter its place at the end of a calculus book, because it connects a sum to an integral — and it does so with the very rectangle-and-curve picture you built in Chapters 10 and 11.

Take a series ∑f(n)\sum f(n) whose terms come from a function ff that is positive, decreasing, and continuous. Draw the terms as rectangles of width 11 and height f(n)f(n), and lay them against the graph of ff. Because ff is decreasing, each rectangle is trapped between the area just under and just over the curve — and summing across all of them traps the whole sum between two copies of the same integral:

∑n=2∞f(n)≤∫1∞f(x)dx≤∑n=1∞f(n).\sum_{n=2}^\infty f(n) \;\le\; \int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx \;\le\; \sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n).

The sum and the integral differ by at most the single term f(1)f(1), so they are finite together or infinite together. That is the test:

The integral test. For positive, decreasing, continuous ff, the series ∑n=1∞f(n)\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty f(n) and the integral ∫1∞f(x)dx\displaystyle\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx either both converge or both diverge.

Look at what just happened: the integral you learned to compute with the Fundamental Theorem now decides infinite sums. The rectangles that defined the integral in Chapter 10 reappear here as the terms of a series, and the area they approximate settles whether the sum is finite. Adding up infinitely many numbers and measuring the area under a curve are, once again, the same question. (Figure 12.3: the curve y=f(x)y = f(x) with the series’ rectangles drawn against it — one set tucked under the curve, one rising above — visibly squeezing the sum to the area.)

The p-series, settled at last — and a debt from Chapter 11 repaid

Apply the integral test to the family ∑n=1∞1np\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^p}. Convergence hinges on ∫1∞1xpdx\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^p}\,dx — which we computed in Chapter 11: it is finite exactly when p>1p > 1. Therefore:

∑n=1∞1np\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^p} converges if and only if p>1p > 1.

This resolves the cliffhanger Chapter 11 left you with. There, ∫1∞1xdx\int_1^\infty \tfrac1x\,dx was infinite while ∫1∞1x2dx\int_1^\infty \tfrac{1}{x^2}\,dx was finite — and you were asked to predict which of ∑1n\sum\tfrac1n and ∑1n2\sum\tfrac1{n^2} converges. Now you know, and you know why they match: the harmonic series (p=1p=1) diverges because its integral does; ∑1n2\sum \tfrac{1}{n^2} (p=2p=2) converges because its integral does. (That sum, remarkably, equals π26\tfrac{\pi^2}{6} — a fact whose proof waits for the Fourier methods of Book 3.) The sum and the area live or die together, exactly as Chapter 11 promised.

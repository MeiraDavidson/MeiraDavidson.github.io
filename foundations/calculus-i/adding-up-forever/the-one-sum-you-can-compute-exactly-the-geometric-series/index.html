The one sum you can compute exactly: the geometric series

Almost no series can be summed in closed form. One family can, and it’s the benchmark for everything else — and you’ve already met it, as the Taylor series of 11−x\tfrac{1}{1-x} in Chapter 7. The geometric series adds powers of a fixed ratio rr:

∑n=0∞rn=1+r+r2+r3+⋯.\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n = 1 + r + r^2 + r^3 + \cdots.

Its partial sum has a clean form (multiply SN=1+r+⋯+rNS_N = 1 + r + \cdots + r^N by rr and subtract): SN=1−rN+11−rS_N = \dfrac{1 - r^{N+1}}{1 - r}. Now take N→∞N \to \infty. If |r|<1|r| < 1, then rN+1→0r^{N+1} \to 0 and the sum converges:

∑n=0∞rn=11−r(|r|<1).\boxed{\;\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n = \frac{1}{1-r} \quad (|r| < 1).\;}

If |r|≥1|r| \ge 1, the terms don’t shrink and the partial sums run away — divergent. So 12+14+⋯=11−1/2=1\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{1-1/2} = 1 ✓, while 1+2+4+⋯1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots explodes. This is precisely Chapter 7’s 11−x=1+x+x2+⋯\tfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots with x=rx = r, and the condition |r|<1|r|<1 is the same “wall at ±1\pm 1” you saw the series slam into there. The geometric series is the ruler we measure other series against.

A first sanity check: the terms must die

Here is the simplest possible test, and the one principle you can always apply first.

The term test. If ∑an\displaystyle\sum a_n converges, then an→0a_n \to 0.

The reason is one line: an=Sn−Sn−1a_n = S_n - S_{n-1}, and if both partial sums converge to the same SS, their difference goes to S−S=0S - S = 0. The useful form is the contrapositive: if the terms do not go to zero, the series diverges. So ∑nn+1\sum \tfrac{n}{n+1} diverges instantly (its terms approach 11, not 00) — no further work needed.

But beware the trap that ensnares everyone at first: the term test is necessary, not sufficient. Terms going to zero does not guarantee convergence. The most important example in the subject proves it.

The harmonic series: every term vanishes, yet the sum is infinite

Consider ∑n=1∞1n=1+12+13+14+⋯\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac1n = 1 + \frac12 + \frac13 + \frac14 + \cdots. The terms 1n\tfrac1n shrink to zero, so the term test is passed — and yet this series diverges to infinity. This is one of the genuine surprises of mathematics, and a medieval one: the argument is due to Nicole Oresme, around 1350, and it is pure cleverness.

◆ Layer 2 — Oresme’s proof that the harmonic series diverges

Group the terms in blocks of doubling length and bound each block from below: 1+12+(13+14)⏟>14+14=12+(15+16+17+18)⏟>4⋅18=12+(19+⋯+116)⏟>8⋅116=12+⋯1 + \frac12 + \underbrace{\left(\frac13 + \frac14\right)}_{> \,\frac14 + \frac14 = \frac12} + \underbrace{\left(\frac15 + \frac16 + \frac17 + \frac18\right)}_{>\, 4\cdot\frac18 = \frac12} + \underbrace{\left(\frac19 + \cdots + \frac1{16}\right)}_{>\, 8\cdot\frac1{16} = \frac12} + \cdots Every block exceeds 12\tfrac12 (each has terms no smaller than its last, and there are exactly enough of them). Since there are infinitely many blocks, each contributing more than 12\tfrac12, the partial sums climb past 12,1,32,2,…\tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, 2, \ldots without bound. The sum is infinite. ◼\blacksquare

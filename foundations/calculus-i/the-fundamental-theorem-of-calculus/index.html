Calculus I

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Or: why finding an area is the same as undoing a derivative — the summit of the book

Companion notebook. Play with this chapter’s ideas — sliders, animations, and drills — in notebooks/ch11_fundamental_theorem.ipynb.

This is the chapter everything has been climbing toward. In Chapter 10 you built two things and left them estranged: the definite integral, a signed area defined as a punishing limit of rectangle-sums, and the antiderivative, found in seconds by running the Chapter 4 rules backward. They share a symbol and seem to share nothing else. This chapter proves they are the same — that the area under a curve can be computed by undoing a derivative — and that single fact is the most consequential theorem in all of calculus, perhaps in all of the mathematics that science uses.

By the end you will be able to evaluate a definite integral in one line instead of a page; you’ll learn integration by parts, the product rule run backward; you’ll see how every “total” — an area between curves, a volume, the length of a curve — is the same slicing-and-summing idea, so you never have to memorize a formula you can rederive; and, as the book’s final act, you will define the logarithm as an integral and watch every loan we took on about ee and ln\ln — taken on faith chapters ago — get paid in full.

The whole chapter rests on one picture: let the right edge of an area move, and watch how fast the area grows.

The bridge, discovered: a moving area

Take a continuous function ff and define the area-so-far function

A(x)=∫axf(t)dt,A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt,

the signed area under ff from the fixed left edge aa out to a movable right edge xx. (You met this in Chapter 10’s last problem, where A(x)=∫0xt2dtA(x) = \int_0^x t^2\,dt quietly turned out to have derivative x2x^2.) Now ask the central question: how fast does this area grow as you slide xx to the right? — that is, what is A′(x)A'(x)?

Push the right edge out by a tiny amount hh. The area gains a thin vertical strip of width hh and height about f(x)f(x), so the extra area is approximately f(x)⋅hf(x)\cdot h:

A(x+h)−A(x)≈f(x)⋅h⇒A(x+h)−A(x)h≈f(x).A(x+h) - A(x) \approx f(x)\cdot h \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h} \approx f(x).

Let h→0h \to 0 and the approximation becomes exact. The left side is the definition of A′(x)A'(x). So A′(x)=f(x)A'(x) = f(x): the rate at which area accumulates is exactly the height of the curve. (Figure 11.1: the region under ff from aa to xx, with the thin strip of width hh and height f(x)f(x) added on at the right edge — the strip whose area is A(x+h)−A(x)A(x+h)-A(x).)

That is the entire secret. Differentiating the area gives back the curve. Read it the other way and it says the area function is an antiderivative of ff — which is the bridge between the two faces.

◆ Layer 2 — The strip argument, made rigorous

“The strip has height about f(x)f(x)” needs tightening. On the little interval [x,x+h][x, x+h], the continuous function ff has a minimum value mhm_h and a maximum MhM_h (Extreme Value Theorem, Chapter 2). The strip’s area A(x+h)−A(x)A(x+h)-A(x) is squeezed between the areas of the short and tall rectangles: mh⋅h≤A(x+h)−A(x)≤Mh⋅h.m_h\cdot h \;\le\; A(x+h) - A(x) \;\le\; M_h\cdot h. Divide by hh: mh≤A(x+h)−A(x)h≤Mh\;m_h \le \tfrac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \le M_h. As h→0h\to0 the interval shrinks to the point xx, and by continuity both mhm_h and MhM_h converge to f(x)f(x). The middle is squeezed (Chapter 2’s Squeeze Theorem) to f(x)f(x). Hence A′(x)=f(x)A'(x) = f(x), exactly. ◼\blacksquare

This is half of the theorem.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1. If ff is continuous and A(x)=∫axf(t)dtA(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, then AA is differentiable and A′(x)=f(x).A'(x) = f(x). Every continuous function is the derivative of its own accumulated area.

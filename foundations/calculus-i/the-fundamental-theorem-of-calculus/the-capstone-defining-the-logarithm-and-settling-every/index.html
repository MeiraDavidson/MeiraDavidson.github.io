The capstone: defining the logarithm, and settling every debt

We end by paying our debts. All through this book we borrowed: in Chapter 1 we asserted the number ee exists; in Chapter 5 we used ln⁡′x=1x\ln' x = \tfrac1x and the laws of logarithms; each time we flagged it as a loan against a later, rigorous account. This is that account. And the instrument that settles everything is the one antiderivative the power rule could never produce — the missing n=−1n=-1 case, ∫1xdx\int \tfrac1x\,dx.

So we turn it around and make it a definition:

ln⁡x:=∫1x1tdt(x>0).\boxed{\;\ln x \;:=\; \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt \quad (x > 0).\;}

We define the natural logarithm to be the area under 1t\tfrac1t from 11 to xx. From this one line, with nothing assumed, every property of ln\ln we ever used falls out as a theorem:

  • ln⁡1=0\ln 1 = 0: it’s ∫111tdt\int_1^1 \tfrac1t\,dt, an integral over no width. ✓ (The loan that ln⁡1=0\ln 1 = 0 — repaid by definition.)
  • ln⁡′x=1x\ln' x = \tfrac1x: immediate from the Fundamental Theorem, Part 1 — the derivative of ∫1x1tdt\int_1^x \tfrac1t\,dt is the integrand 1x\tfrac1x. The fact we assumed in Chapter 5 is now a one-line consequence. ✓
  • ln\ln is increasing for x>0x>0: its derivative 1x\tfrac1x is positive. And it grows without bound as x→∞x\to\infty (the area under 1t\tfrac1t never stops accumulating). ✓
  • The product law ln⁡(ab)=ln⁡a+ln⁡b\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b: split the defining integral and substitute. We have ln⁡(ab)=∫1ab1tdt=∫1a1tdt+∫aab1tdt\ln(ab) = \int_1^{ab}\tfrac1t dt = \int_1^a \tfrac1t dt + \int_a^{ab}\tfrac1t dt. In the second piece let u=t/au = t/a (so dt=adudt = a\,du, and t:a→abt:a\to ab becomes u:1→bu:1\to b): it turns into ∫1b1udu=ln⁡b\int_1^b \tfrac{1}{u}\,du = \ln b. Hence ln⁡(ab)=ln⁡a+ln⁡b\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b — the signature property of logarithms, emerging from integral additivity and a substitution. ✓
  • The number ee exists: define ee as the unique number with ln⁡e=1\ln e = 1 — i.e. the right edge at which the area under 1t\tfrac1t first equals 11. It exists because ln\ln is continuous with ln⁡1=0<1\ln 1 = 0 < 1 and grows past 11, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem (Chapter 2) it hits 11 somewhere. This is the very loan from Chapter 1 — that ee exists at all — now discharged completely. ✓
  • exe^x and its derivative: the exponential is simply the inverse function of ln\ln, and ddxex=ex\tfrac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x follows from the inverse-function rule (Chapter 4). ✓

Every promissory note the book ever wrote is now paid. The whole edifice — ee, ln\ln, exe^x, their derivatives and laws — which we used freely and honestly flagged as borrowed, rests in the end on a single integral and the Fundamental Theorem. The account balances.

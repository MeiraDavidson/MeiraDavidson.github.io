Calculus I · The Mean Value Theorem

Now tilt it: the Mean Value Theorem

Rolle handled the flat case, where the endpoints are level. The Mean Value Theorem handles every case by a trick so good it’s almost cheating: tilt the whole picture until the endpoints are level, apply Rolle, then tilt back.

Mean Value Theorem. If ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), then there is at least one point cc in (a,b)(a,b) where f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a.f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.

The right side is the average rate of change over the whole interval — the slope of the straight “secant” line connecting the two endpoints of the graph. The theorem says some interior tangent line is parallel to that secant: at the instant cc, your instantaneous rate equals your trip average. (Figure 6.2: the secant line through the endpoints, and a parallel tangent line touching the curve at cc.)

◆ Proof of the MVT

Subtract off the secant line to flatten the endpoints. Define g(x)=f(x)−[f(a)+f(b)−f(a)b−a(x−a)].g(x) = f(x) - \left[\,f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,(x - a)\right]. The bracket is exactly the secant line, so gg measures how far ff sits above or below it. Check the endpoints: g(a)=f(a)−f(a)=0g(a) = f(a) - f(a) = 0, and g(b)=f(b)−f(b)=0g(b) = f(b) - f(b) = 0. So g(a)=g(b)g(a) = g(b), and gg inherits continuity and differentiability from ff. Rolle applies to gg: there’s a cc with g′(c)=0g'(c) = 0. But g′(x)=f′(x)−f(b)−f(a)b−a,g'(x) = f'(x) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}, so g′(c)=0g'(c) = 0 means precisely f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−af'(c) = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. ◼\blacksquare

Notice what just happened: the general theorem cost us almost nothing once we had the special one, because subtracting a straight line changes slopes by a constant and converts “average slope” into “zero.” That move — straighten the problem, solve the easy version, un-straighten — is one you’ll use for the rest of your mathematical life.

Why this chapter exists: three consequences

The MVT is a tool, and here is what it builds. Each of these is short to prove now and impossible to prove without it.

Consequence 1 — Zero slope means constant (the Chapter 5 debt, repaid)

If f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0 at every point of an interval, then ff is constant on that interval.

Proof. Take any two points x<yx < y in the interval. By the MVT, f(y)−f(x)=f′(c)(y−x)f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)\,(y - x) for some cc between them. But f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0, so f(y)−f(x)=0f(y) - f(x) = 0, i.e. f(y)=f(x)f(y) = f(x). Every two points have the same value: ff is constant. ◼\blacksquare

This looks almost too obvious to need proving — of course a function that never changes is constant. But “never has slope” and “never changes value” are genuinely different statements, and the only bridge between them is the MVT. Back in Chapter 5 we wanted to show CekxCe^{kx} was the solution to dNdt=kN\frac{dN}{dt} = kN, not merely a solution, and the whole argument hinged on “g′(x)=0g'(x) = 0 everywhere, therefore gg is constant.” We used it on faith. That loan is now paid in full.

A twin fact comes for free and will run all of integration: if f′(x)=g′(x)f'(x) = g'(x) everywhere, then ff and gg differ by a constant (apply Consequence 1 to f−gf - g). When you reach Chapter 10 and wonder where the “+C+C” in an antiderivative comes from — this is its birth certificate.

Consequence 2 — The sign of the derivative controls rising and falling

If f′(x)>0f'(x) > 0 on an interval, then ff is increasing there. If f′(x)<0f'(x) < 0, then ff is decreasing.

Proof. Take x<yx < y. The MVT gives f(y)−f(x)=f′(c)(y−x)f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x) with y−x>0y - x > 0. If f′(c)>0f'(c) > 0 the right side is positive, so f(y)>f(x)f(y) > f(x): increasing. If f′(c)<0f'(c) < 0, it’s negative, so f(y)<f(x)f(y) < f(x): decreasing. ◼\blacksquare

This is the one that earns its keep in Chapter 8. The entire method of finding maxima and minima — locate where f′f' changes sign, because that’s where ff turns around — is just this consequence, read carefully. Without it, “set the derivative to zero” would be a ritual with no justification. With it, it’s a theorem.

Consequence 3 — A function can’t outrun its steepest slope

If |f′(x)|≤M|f'(x)| \le M everywhere on [a,b][a,b], then |f(b)−f(a)|≤M(b−a)|f(b) - f(a)| \le M\,(b - a).

Proof. By the MVT, f(b)−f(a)=f′(c)(b−a)f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b-a), so |f(b)−f(a)|=|f′(c)|(b−a)≤M(b−a)|f(b) - f(a)| = |f'(c)|\,(b-a) \le M(b-a). ◼\blacksquare

This is the rigorous version of the robustness idea from Chapter 2: a function whose slope is capped simply cannot change too much over a short interval. It is the reason numerical methods come with error guarantees — bound the derivative, and you’ve bounded how wrong you can be. Engineers live on inequalities like this one.

◆ Layer 2 — The generalized MVT, and a glimpse of Taylor’s engine

There’s a two-function version of the MVT, due to Cauchy, that looks technical but is the secret machinery behind Chapter 7’s Taylor remainder (and behind L’Hôpital’s rule, if you meet it).

Cauchy’s MVT. If ff and gg are continuous on [a,b][a,b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), there is a cc in (a,b)(a,b) with [f(b)−f(a)]g′(c)=[g(b)−g(a)]f′(c).\big[f(b) - f(a)\big]\,g'(c) = \big[g(b) - g(a)\big]\,f'(c). Proof (same tilt trick, cleverer line). Apply Rolle to h(x)=[f(b)−f(a)]g(x)−[g(b)−g(a)]f(x)h(x) = \big[f(b)-f(a)\big]g(x) - \big[g(b)-g(a)\big]f(x). A quick check gives h(a)=h(b)h(a) = h(b) (the cross terms cancel), so some cc has h′(c)=0h'(c) = 0, which is exactly the claim. ◼\blacksquare

Why care now? The ordinary MVT says f(b)=f(a)+f′(c)(b−a)f(b) = f(a) + f'(c)(b-a) — it pins down ff at bb using one derivative and an unknown midpoint cc. Taylor’s theorem will say f(b)=f(a)+f′(a)(b−a)+12f″(c)(b−a)2f(b) = f(a) + f'(a)(b-a) + \tfrac12 f''(c)(b-a)^2 — the same shape, one derivative deeper, and Cauchy’s MVT is what proves that error term has exactly that form. The MVT isn’t just a theorem in this book; it’s the first rung of a ladder.

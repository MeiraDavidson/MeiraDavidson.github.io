First, the easy case: Rolle’s theorem

Start with a picture so simple it feels like it can’t be saying much. Suppose a smooth curve starts and ends at the same height — f(a)=f(b)f(a) = f(b). Draw any such curve in the air with your finger. To come back down to where you started, you had to stop climbing at some point and turn around: there is a peak, or a valley, somewhere strictly between the ends. And at that turning point, the curve is momentarily flat — its slope is zero.

That is the entire content of Rolle’s theorem.

Rolle’s Theorem. If ff is continuous on [a,b][a,b], differentiable on (a,b)(a,b), and f(a)=f(b)f(a) = f(b), then there is at least one point cc in (a,b)(a,b) where f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0.

The proof rests on one small, sharp fact about what the derivative must do at a peak — a fact worth isolating, because we will reuse it when we hunt for maxima in Chapter 8.

◆ Fermat’s lemma: at an interior peak, the slope is zero

Claim. If ff has a local maximum at an interior point cc (and f′(c)f'(c) exists), then f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0. (Same for a local minimum.)

Why. Look at the difference quotient f(c+h)−f(c)h\dfrac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} near the peak. Because cc is a maximum, the numerator f(c+h)−f(c)f(c+h) - f(c) is ≤0\le 0 for every small hh — you can’t go higher than the top. Now watch the sign of the whole quotient from each side: - From the right (h>0h > 0): numerator ≤0\le 0, denominator >0> 0, so the quotient is ≤0\le 0. Taking h→0+h\to0^+ gives f′(c)≤0f'(c) \le 0. - From the left (h<0h < 0): numerator ≤0\le 0, denominator <0< 0, so the quotient is ≥0\ge 0. Taking h→0−h\to0^- gives f′(c)≥0f'(c) \ge 0.

The derivative exists, so both one-sided limits equal f′(c)f'(c). The only number that is both ≤0\le 0 and ≥0\ge 0 is 00. So f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0. ◼\blacksquare

With that in hand, Rolle falls out cleanly:

◆ Proof of Rolle’s theorem

By the Extreme Value Theorem, ff attains a maximum and a minimum somewhere on [a,b][a,b]. Two cases. - If both the max and the min happen at the endpoints, then since f(a)=f(b)f(a) = f(b), the highest and lowest values of ff are equal — so ff is constant, and f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0 at every interior cc. - Otherwise, at least one of them occurs at an interior point cc. By Fermat’s lemma, f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0 there.

Either way, some interior cc has f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0. ◼\blacksquare

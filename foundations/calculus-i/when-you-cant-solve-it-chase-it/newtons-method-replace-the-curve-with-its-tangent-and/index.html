Newton’s method: replace the curve with its tangent, and repeat

We want a root of ff — a place x⋆x_\star where f(x⋆)=0f(x_\star) = 0. Suppose we have a guess xnx_n that is in the neighborhood but not exact. From Chapter 6 we know that near xnx_n, the function is almost equal to its tangent line:

f(x)≈f(xn)+f′(xn)(x−xn).f(x) \approx f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(x - x_n).

The curve is hard. The tangent line is easy — it is just a straight line, and a straight line crosses zero in exactly one obvious place. So here is the whole idea: instead of asking where the hard curve hits zero, ask where its easy tangent line hits zero, and use that as our next guess. Set the right-hand side to 00 and call the solution xn+1x_{n+1}:

0=f(xn)+f′(xn)(xn+1−xn).0 = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(x_{n+1} - x_n).

Solve for xn+1x_{n+1} — just rearranging:

xn+1−xn=−f(xn)f′(xn)⇒xn+1=xn−f(xn)f′(xn)x_{n+1} - x_n = -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\;}

That is Newton’s method. You did not memorize it; you built it. Geometrically: stand on the curve above your guess, slide down the tangent line until it hits the xx-axis, and step over to that spot. The new spot is almost always closer to the true root. Then do it again. (Figure 9.1: the curve, the tangent at x0x_0 sliding to the axis at x1x_1, the tangent at x1x_1 sliding to x2x_2, the guesses marching in toward the root.)

Watching it work: computing 2\sqrt{2} by hand

In Book 1, the very first irrational number we met was 2\sqrt{2} — a number with no exact fraction and no terminating decimal. We could prove it existed but we could not write it down. Now we can chase it.

Finding 2\sqrt{2} means finding the root of f(x)=x2−2f(x) = x^2 - 2 (since x2−2=0x^2 - 2 = 0 means x=2x = \sqrt 2). Its derivative is f′(x)=2xf'(x) = 2x. Drop these into Newton’s formula:

xn+1=xn−xn2−22xn=xn2+1xn=12(xn+2xn).x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - 2}{2x_n} = \frac{x_n}{2} + \frac{1}{x_n} = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(x_n + \frac{2}{x_n}\right).

Start with a rough guess, x0=1.5x_0 = 1.5, and turn the crank:

step value correct digits
x0x_0 1.51.5 —
x1x_1 1.416666…1.416\,666\ldots ≈2\approx 2
x2x_2 1.4142157…1.414\,215\,7\ldots ≈5\approx 5
x3x_3 1.4142135623…1.414\,213\,562\,3\ldots ≈11\approx 11

The true value is 2=1.41421356237…\sqrt 2 = 1.414\,213\,562\,37\ldots. Look at the last column: the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step — two, then five, then eleven. After three steps from a sloppy start we have eleven correct digits. This blistering speed is called quadratic convergence, and it is Newton’s signature. (That formula xn+1=12(xn+2/xn)x_{n+1} = \frac12(x_n + 2/x_n), by the way, is the square-root method the Babylonians used four thousand years ago. They had no calculus; they found the rule by cleverness. You just rediscovered it from a tangent line.)

Toolkit: Newton’s method (finding a root)

To solve f(x)=0f(x) = 0 when no formula will: 1. Pick a starting guess x0x_0 somewhere near the root. 2. Repeat: xn+1=xn−f(xn)f′(xn)\quad x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. 3. Stop when the guesses stop changing (to as many digits as you need).

What it’s really doing: approximating the curve by its tangent line and jumping to where the line hits zero. Fast — digits roughly double each step — when it works. The next section is about when it doesn’t.

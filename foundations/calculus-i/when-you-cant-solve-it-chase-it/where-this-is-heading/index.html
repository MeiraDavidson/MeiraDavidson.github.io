Calculus I · When You Can’t Solve It, Chase It

Where this is heading

Everything in this chapter happened on a number line — xx was a single number, f′f' a single slope, “downhill” a choice between left and right. In Book 3, xx becomes a vector — a point in a space of many dimensions — and the derivative f′f' becomes the gradient, a vector that points in the steepest uphill direction. “Step against the slope” becomes “step against the gradient,” and the update rule keeps the exact same shape: xn+1=xn−η(gradient at xn)x_{n+1} = x_n - \eta\,(\text{gradient at } x_n). The picture you built here — a ball rolling to the bottom of a valley, a learning rate that must be neither too timid nor too bold — is the whole picture. Book 3 just gives the ball more directions it is allowed to roll.

When you eventually watch a neural network train and see its “loss” tick downward step by step, you will not be watching magic. You will be watching this chapter, run a few million times.

Problems

This chapter is about iteration, so its “doing” is mostly computational — you learn gradient descent by running it and watching it converge or blow up. So the tiers are shaped differently here. The Computational Lab is the heart of the practice: structured experiments in the companion notebook. By hand is the short list genuinely worth doing on paper. Concept tests judgment. ◆ Diamond is where this chapter’s real paper depth lives — the proofs are the practice. Teach it and Looking ahead close it out.

Computational Lab — the real practice; run these in the notebook

  1. Learning-rate sweep. On f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2, run gradient descent from x0=1x_0=1 for η=0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9,1.0,1.1,1.5\eta=0.1,\,0.3,\,0.5,\,0.7,\,0.9,\,1.0,\,1.1,\,1.5. Plot xnx_n versus nn for each. Sort them into four behaviors (smooth glide, shrinking spiral, stuck bounce, explosion) and check the boundary against the prediction |1−2η|<1|1-2\eta|<1.
  2. Hunt the fastest rate. Still on x2x^2: which η\eta reaches within 10−610^{-6} of the minimum in the fewest steps? Compare your experimental winner with the predicted optimum η=12\eta=\tfrac12.
  3. Newton, fast and broken. Implement xn+1=12(xn+2xn)x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+\tfrac{2}{x_n}) for 2\sqrt2; print xnx_n and the error |xn−2||x_n-\sqrt2| each step and watch the correct digits roughly double. Then point Newton at f(x)=x1/3f(x)=x^{1/3} from x0=1x_0=1 and watch it fly apart.
  4. A landscape with two valleys. Run gradient descent on f(x)=x4−3x2+xf(x)=x^4-3x^2+x from several starting points. Show that different starts land in different minima. (You’ve just discovered, by experiment, the central headache of training: gradient descent finds a local minimum, not necessarily the best one — and which one depends on where you begin.)
  5. The race. On a single quadratic, count the steps gradient descent (good η\eta) and Newton’s method each need to reach a fixed tolerance. Watch Newton win by a landslide — then find a setup where Newton fails and gradient descent doesn’t.
  6. (Stretch) A first real optimizer. Add “momentum”: xn+1=xn−ηf′(xn)+β(xn−xn−1)x_{n+1}=x_n-\eta f'(x_n)+\beta\,(x_n-x_{n-1}) with β=0.9\beta=0.9. On a stretched bowl f(x)=10x2f(x)=10x^2, compare plain gradient descent against the momentum version. (This is a baby version of the optimizers that actually train neural networks.)

By hand — worth doing on paper

  1. For f(x)=x2−5f(x)=x^2-5: write f′f', write Newton’s rule, simplify it, then do two steps from x0=2x_0=2 toward 5\sqrt5.
  2. Gradient descent on f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 with η=0.1\eta=0.1, x0=1x_0=1: compute x1,x2,x3x_1,x_2,x_3.
  3. For f(x)=x2−4x+7f(x)=x^2-4x+7: find f′f', find the exact minimizer from f′=0f'=0, then take two gradient-descent steps from x0=0x_0=0 with η=0.1\eta=0.1 and check you’re heading the right way.
  4. Classify the behavior for each factor 1−2η1-2\eta: η=0.2,0.5,0.8,1.2\eta=0.2,\ 0.5,\ 0.8,\ 1.2 (monotone glide / one-step / oscillating glide / explosion).

Concept & counterexample — think; true/false with justification, or build the case

    1. A smaller learning rate is always safer, so always better. (b) Gradient descent can overshoot the minimum. (c) Newton’s method always converges faster than gradient descent. (d) If gradient descent’s steps keep shrinking, it must be approaching the minimum.
  2. Predict, then check. For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2 with η=0.5\eta=0.5, where is x1x_1 — without computing step by step? For η=1\eta=1, describe the long-run behavior in one sentence.
  3. When it breaks. Give (a) a function/start where Newton diverges, and (b) a function/start where gradient descent diverges, naming the cause of each. Which failure is fixable by changing only one number, and which isn’t?
  4. The two-valley trap (on paper). Sketch a function with a shallow valley and a deeper one. Mark a start that gradient descent sends to the shallow (worse) minimum and one that sends it to the deeper one. In one sentence, what does this say about “gradient descent finds the best answer”?
  5. An asymmetry worth seeing. For f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2, argue that too small an η\eta only makes you slow (you still converge), while too large an η\eta makes you fail outright. Why are the two kinds of “wrong learning rate” not symmetric?

◆ Diamond — the proofs are the practice

  1. ◆ Derive Newton’s method from the tangent-line approximation f(x)≈f(xn)+f′(xn)(x−xn)f(x)\approx f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x-x_n): set the line to zero and solve for xn+1x_{n+1}.
  2. ◆ Show Newton on f(x)=x2−Af(x)=x^2-A gives the Babylonian rule xn+1=12(xn+Axn)x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+\tfrac{A}{x_n}), and use it to compute 7\sqrt7 to five decimals from x0=3x_0=3.
  3. ◆ The cube-root disaster: for f(x)=x1/3f(x)=x^{1/3}, show Newton’s rule simplifies to xn+1=−2xnx_{n+1}=-2x_n; conclude divergence from every nonzero start and explain geometrically (what is the tangent near x=0x=0 doing?).
  4. ◆ The learning-rate range, in general. For f(x)=a2(x−m)2f(x)=\tfrac{a}{2}(x-m)^2 (a bowl of curvature a>0a>0, minimum at mm), show gradient descent gives xn+1−m=(1−ηa)(xn−m)x_{n+1}-m=(1-\eta a)(x_n-m), hence xn−m=(1−ηa)n(x0−m)x_n-m=(1-\eta a)^n(x_0-m). Find the exact convergence range and the optimal η\eta. Then state the single most important practical lesson: what must happen to the safe learning rate as the curvature aa grows?
  5. ◆◆ Why Newton’s error squares. For xn+1=12(xn+Axn)x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+\tfrac{A}{x_n}), let en=xn−Ae_n=x_n-\sqrt A. Prove the exact identity en+1=en22xne_{n+1}=\dfrac{e_n^2}{2x_n}. Conclude that near the root the error roughly squares each step — which is exactly why the correct digits double (quadratic convergence).
  6. ◆◆ Newton reaches a quadratic’s minimum in one step. Using xn+1=xn−f′(xn)f″(xn)x_{n+1}=x_n-\tfrac{f'(x_n)}{f''(x_n)}, show that for any f(x)=ax2+bx+cf(x)=ax^2+bx+c (a>0a>0) the first step lands on the exact minimizer, regardless of x0x_0. Explain via Chapter 6 why this had to happen (how well does a quadratic match its own second-order Taylor model?).
  7. ◆◆ The fixed-point view that unifies everything. Both methods have the form xn+1=g(xn)x_{n+1}=g(x_n), and a limit LL must satisfy g(L)=Lg(L)=L — a fixed point, exactly like Chapter 1’s recursive sequences. A general fact (which you may use): the iteration converges to LL when |g′(L)|<1|g'(L)|<1, and the smaller |g′(L)||g'(L)|, the faster. (a) For gradient descent, g(x)=x−ηf′(x)g(x)=x-\eta f'(x); compute g′(L)g'(L) at a minimum LL and recover the convergence range from problem 19. (b) For Newton, g(x)=x−f(x)f′(x)g(x)=x-\tfrac{f(x)}{f'(x)}; show g′(L)=0g'(L)=0 at a simple root. Explain how this one criterion accounts for both the learning-rate range and Newton’s blazing speed.

Teach it — explain it, write it up, or fix it

  1. In three or four sentences with the ball-in-a-valley picture, teach a friend what the learning rate is and what goes wrong at both extremes — too big and too small.
  2. Debug your classmate. They say: “Newton’s method is faster, so real AI should obviously use it instead of gradient descent.” Give the one true sentence that explains why neural networks are trained with gradient descent anyway.
  3. Write a worked solution for “use Newton’s method to find 10\sqrt{10} to four decimals,” narrating the setup, the rule, and each iteration so a peer could reuse it for any square root.
  4. Design a problem. Cook up a specific quadratic ff and a learning rate η\eta for which gradient descent reaches the minimum in exactly one step. Show why your η\eta works (relate it to f″f''), and write the solution key.
  5. In a few sentences, teach the connection between “finding where f=0f=0” (Newton’s first job) and “finding where ff is smallest” (gradient descent’s job) — how does the same machine do both?

Looking ahead — Book 3, where xx becomes a vector

  1. In Book 3, xx becomes a vector and f′f' becomes the gradient — a vector pointing in the steepest uphill direction. Predict what the rule xn+1=xn−ηf′(xn)x_{n+1}=x_n-\eta f'(x_n) becomes, and what stays exactly the same.
  2. Your safe range was 0<η<2f″0<\eta<\tfrac{2}{f''}. In many dimensions there isn’t one curvature f″f'' but many — different in different directions. Predict why a single number η\eta might be too big in one direction and too small in another at the same time. (This is why advanced optimizers adapt the rate per direction.)
  3. Newton needs f″f'': one number here, an N×NN\times N table in NN dimensions. For a model with one million parameters, how many entries is that table? Say in one sentence why that number ends Newton’s method at real scale — and reconnect it to this chapter’s punchline.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. Most equations worth caring about have no formula for the exact answer. The honest move is a guess that improves every repetition. Why is that an uncomfortable shift for someone taught that maths gives exact answers?
  2. The learning rate is a single dial that decides whether gradient descent glides down, bounces, or flies off the edge. Where else does one setting control the difference between working and failing?
  3. Newton's method needs a table of second derivatives — a million-by-million table for a million-parameter model. Say in one sentence why that ends the method at real scale.

Calculus I · Getting Arbitrarily Close

From a marching nn to a sliding xx

We write

limx→af(x)=L\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L

to mean: as xx gets arbitrarily close to aa — closing in from the left and from the right — the value f(x)f(x) gets and stays arbitrarily close to LL. Everything you earned in Chapter 1 carries straight over: the limit laws (sums, products, quotients of limits behave as you’d hope), the squeeze idea, and the challenge–response game behind the word “arbitrarily.” We are lifting the same machinery from a discrete staircase to a continuous slide.

For most functions, at most points, this is gloriously boring. The function f(x)=x2+2f(x)=x^2+2 heads toward 1111 as x→3x\to3, for the unsurprising reason that f(3)=11f(3)=11. When a function is well-behaved, the limit is just the value, and you find it by substitution. The interesting cases — the ones we built this whole subject to handle — are exactly the cases where substitution fails.

The point you’re approaching might not be on the graph

Look at

f(x)=x2−1x−1.f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}.

Try to evaluate it at x=1x = 1 by substitution: you get 00\tfrac{0}{0}, which is not a number. The function is undefined at x=1x=1 — there is a literal hole in its graph there. A careless reader concludes “no value, no limit, move on.” Watch what actually happens.

For every x≠1x \ne 1, we are allowed to cancel, because the thing we’re canceling isn’t zero:

x2−1x−1=(x−1)(x+1)x−1=x+1.\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x + 1.

So everywhere except the single point x=1x=1, this function is identical to the simple line x+1x+1. And x+1x+1 obviously heads toward 22 as x→1x\to1. Therefore

limx→1x2−1x−1=2,\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = 2,

even though f(1)f(1) flat-out does not exist. Read that again. The limit is 22 at a point where the function has no value at all. This is the loophole promised above, stated plainly: the limit listens to what the function does on the approach, and the destination point itself is allowed to be missing. (Figure 2.1: the line y=x+1y=x+1 with a small open circle punched out at (1,2)(1,2) — the hole the function has, that the limit sees right past.)

Hold onto this example with both hands, because it is the entire shape of the next chapter. In Chapter 3, the slope of a curve will be defined as a fraction that is exactly 00\tfrac00 at the point of interest — and we will get a real answer out of it by canceling, in precisely this way. Every derivative you ever compute is a 00\tfrac00 limit tamed by cancellation. You just did your first one.

Calculus I

Getting Arbitrarily Close

Or: What a function is doing right next to a point it might never reach

Companion notebook. Play with this chapter’s ideas — sliders, animations, and drills — in notebooks/ch2_limits.ipynb.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to find what a function approaches as its input slides toward a target — which is the same idea as Chapter 1, with one new and crucial wrinkle. You will compute these limits three ways: by simply plugging in (when the function is well-behaved), by factoring and canceling (when plugging in gives the nonsense 00\tfrac00), and out at infinity. You will know what it means for a function to be continuous — the “draw it without lifting your pen” idea — and why, underneath, continuity is really the promise that a small change in input makes only a small change in output. You’ll also meet the two great theorems that continuity guarantees — the Intermediate Value Theorem (a continuous function can’t skip a value, which is how we’ll prove that solutions exist) and the Extreme Value Theorem (a continuous function on a closed interval actually reaches its highest and lowest points) — both of which the back half of the book quietly runs on.

And you will meet one specific limit, sin⁡xx→1\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\to 1, that looks like it should be undefined and isn’t. It is the single most important limit in the book, because in the very next chapter it turns out to be a slope — your first derivative, computed before we even have the word.

Here is the one new idea, and it is worth saying before anything else. In Chapter 1 the input was a whole number nn that marched off to infinity. Now the input is a real number xx that you slide toward a finite point aa — and it can approach aa from either side. The limit asks what f(x)f(x) is doing in the neighborhood of aa. It does not ask, and does not care, what ff does at aa itself. That gap between “near aa” and “at aa” looks like a technicality. It is actually the loophole that makes all of calculus possible.

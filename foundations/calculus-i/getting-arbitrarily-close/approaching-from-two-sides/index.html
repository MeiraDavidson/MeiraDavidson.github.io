Approaching from two sides

Because xx slides along a line, it can reach aa from the left or from the right, and sometimes the two stories disagree. We write x→a−x\to a^- for the approach from below (the left-hand limit) and x→a+x\to a^+ for the approach from above (the right-hand limit).

limx→af(x)=Lexactly whenlimx→a−f(x)=limx→a+f(x)=L.\boxed{\;\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L \quad\text{exactly when}\quad \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) = L.\;}

The two-sided limit exists only if both one-sided limits exist and agree. The classic disagreement is

f(x)=|x|x,f(x) = \frac{|x|}{x},

which equals +1+1 for every positive xx and −1-1 for every negative xx. As x→0+x\to 0^+ it sits at +1+1; as x→0−x\to 0^- it sits at −1-1. The two sides refuse to meet, so lim⁡x→0|x|x\lim_{x\to 0}\tfrac{|x|}{x} does not exist — not because of a hole, but because of a jump. The function can’t make up its mind, so the limit declines to exist. (Figure 2.2: the two flat arms at +1+1 and −1-1 with the cliff between them at 00.)

Toolkit: Evaluating the limit of a function

Step 1 — try substitution. If ff is well-behaved (continuous) at aa, then lim⁡x→af(x)=f(a)\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a). Done. Always try this first.

Step 2 — if you hit 00\tfrac00, don’t stop — rewrite. The 00\tfrac00 means a common factor is hiding. Two moves flush it out: - Factor and cancel: x2−4x−2=(x−2)(x+2)x−2=x+2→4\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2 \to 4 as x→2x\to2. - Rationalize (when roots appear): multiply top and bottom by the conjugate to clear the square root.

Step 3 — limits at infinity: divide top and bottom by the highest power of xx, exactly as you divided by the highest power of nn in Chapter 1.

Worked example (rationalizing a 00\tfrac00). Find lim⁡x→0x+4−2x\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}. Substituting gives 00\tfrac00. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate x+4+2\sqrt{x+4}+2:

x+4−2x⋅x+4+2x+4+2=(x+4)−4x(x+4+2)=xx(x+4+2)=1x+4+2.\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+4}+2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2} = \frac{(x+4)-4}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)} = \frac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+4}+2\right)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}.

The troublesome xx canceled. Now substitution is safe: as x→0x\to0 this heads to 14+2=14\dfrac{1}{\sqrt4+2}=\dfrac14. (And yes — this too is secretly a derivative, the slope of x+4\sqrt{x+4} at x=0x=0. They’re everywhere once you know the shape.)

Continuity: the graph you can draw without lifting your pen

A function is continuous at aa when the limit and the value finally agree:

limx→af(x)=f(a).\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a).

Three things have to line up: f(a)f(a) has to exist, the limit has to exist, and they have to be equal. When this holds at every point of an interval, you can draw the whole graph there without lifting your pen off the paper — no holes, no jumps, no flying off to infinity. Polynomials are continuous everywhere; so are sin⁡x\sin x, cos⁡x\cos x, and exe^x. The failures come in three flavors, and naming them is useful:

A removable discontinuity is a single missing point — our x2−1x−1\tfrac{x^2-1}{x-1} hole. The limit exists; the function just forgot to take the value. You can “repair” it by defining the missing point to be the limit.

A jump is when the two one-sided limits exist but disagree — our |x|x\tfrac{|x|}{x} cliff. No repair possible; the gap is real.

An infinite discontinuity is when the function blows up, like 1x\tfrac1x at x=0x=0. There’s no finite limit to repair to.

Why an engineer cares

Continuity sounds like a tidiness rule for graphs. It is really a statement about robustness, and that is the first reason this idea reaches far beyond math class. Continuity says: nudge the input a little, and the output moves only a little. Nothing snaps. A continuous control system doesn’t lurch when a sensor reading wobbles; a continuous model doesn’t return a wildly different answer because a measurement was off in the third decimal. Where a system is discontinuous — a circuit breaker tripping, water flashing to steam, a market gapping — is exactly where it behaves unpredictably and where engineers pay closest attention. We will sharpen “a little” into something measurable in the diamond box below, and that sharpening is the same one that makes the whole subject rigorous.

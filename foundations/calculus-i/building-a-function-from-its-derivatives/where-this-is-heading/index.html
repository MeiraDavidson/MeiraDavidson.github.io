Calculus I · Building a Function From Its Derivatives

Where this is heading

Three doors open from here, and you can already see through each.

The second-derivative test (Chapter 8). At a critical point, where f′(a)=0f'(a) = 0, the quadratic approximation collapses to f(x)≈f(a)+12f″(a)(x−a)2f(x) \approx f(a) + \tfrac12 f''(a)(x-a)^2 — a pure parabola. If f″(a)>0f''(a) > 0 it opens upward and aa is a minimum; if f″(a)<0f''(a) < 0 it opens downward and aa is a maximum. The whole second-derivative test is just reading the leading Taylor term at a critical point.

Convexity (Chapter 8). The error of the tangent-line approximation is R1=12f″(c)(x−a)2R_1 = \tfrac12 f''(c)(x-a)^2. When f″≥0f'' \ge 0, that remainder is never negative, so ff never dips below its tangent line — it stays above all of them. A function that stays above all its tangents is convex, and convex is exactly the condition under which gradient descent is guaranteed to find the true bottom. The inequality ex≥1+xe^x \ge 1+x you proved in Chapter 6 was this story’s first instance.

Newton’s quadratic convergence (Chapter 9). Newton’s method replaces ff with its tangent line T1T_1 and solves. The error it leaves behind is governed by the T1T_1 remainder, 12f″(c)(x−a)2\tfrac12 f''(c)(x-a)^2 — proportional to the square of how far off you were. That square is the reason Newton’s correct digits double each step.

A curve is a line, then a parabola, then a polynomial. Once you can choose how close, the rest of calculus is deciding how close you need.

Concept & counterexample — think; true/false with justification, or build the example

    1. TnT_n matches ff and its first nn derivatives at the center. (b) A larger nn always gives a better approximation at every xx. (c) The Taylor series of a polynomial is itself. (d) If two functions share the same Taylor polynomial of every degree at aa, they’re equal. (Part (d) is subtle — see problem 31.)
  2. Find the error. A student writes: “11−x=1+x+x2+⋯\tfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots, so at x=2x = 2, −1=1+2+4+⋯\;-1 = 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots.” Identify the precise mistake.
  3. Explain why sin⁡x\sin x’s series has only odd powers and cos⁡x\cos x’s has only even powers, by connecting each to the function’s symmetry.
  4. Construct a function whose degree-1 Taylor approximation at 00 is exactly 1+3x1 + 3x, then a different one with the same approximation. What do all such functions share, and where do they differ?

  6. Is it possible? A function whose tangent-line error shrinks like (x−a)2(x-a)^2, and another whose error shrinks only like |x−a||x-a|. What property separates them?
  7. The series for ln⁡(1+x)\ln(1+x) is x−x22+⋯x - \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \cdots. Without computing the radius of convergence formally, predict the value of xx at which it must start to fail, and why. (Where does ln⁡(1+x)\ln(1+x) itself misbehave?)

◆ Diamond — the remainder, and the deep identities, are the real content

  1. ◆ Derive the coefficient formula: for P(x)=∑ck(x−a)kP(x)=\sum c_k(x-a)^k, show P(k)(a)=k!ckP^{(k)}(a)=k!\,c_k, hence ck=f(k)(a)k!c_k=\tfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}. (The “everything but one term dies” argument.)
  2. ◆ Prove the quadratic Taylor polynomial is the unique parabola matching ff’s value, slope, and curvature at aa.
  3. ◆ Show the n=0n=0 case of Taylor’s remainder is exactly the Mean Value Theorem, and the n=1n=1 case is the second-order MVT from Chapter 6.

Teach it — explain it, write it up, or fix it

  1. In three or four sentences to a friend, teach “the derivative is the best linear approximation,” and how Taylor extends it to the best quadratic, cubic, and beyond.
  2. Debug your classmate. They write: “ex=1+x+x2+x3+⋯e^x = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots, since every derivative of exe^x is exe^x.” Pinpoint the missing ingredient and give the correct series, with a one-line reason for the 1k!\tfrac1{k!}.
  3. Write a complete worked solution estimating ln⁡(1.1)\ln(1.1) from a Taylor polynomial of ln⁡(1+x)\ln(1+x), with an error bound, narrated so a peer could redo it for ln⁡(1.2)\ln(1.2).
  4. Design a problem. Choose a function (try a trig one) and a center aa, and write a problem asking for its degree-2 (or first three nonzero) Taylor terms, with a clean key.
  5. In a few sentences, teach Euler’s formula to someone who knows the three series but has never seen them combined — what single substitution does all the work, and why is the result surprising?
  6. In a few sentences, teach the difference between a Taylor polynomial (finite, carrying a remainder) and a Taylor series (infinite, equal to the function only where it converges and the remainder vanishes).

Looking ahead — Chapters 8 and 9 in your hands early

  1. At a critical point (f′(a)=0f'(a)=0), the quadratic approximation is f(x)≈f(a)+12f″(a)(x−a)2f(x)\approx f(a)+\tfrac12 f''(a)(x-a)^2. Predict: f″(a)>0f''(a)>0 — min or max? f″(a)<0f''(a)<0? (You’ve derived the second-derivative test.)
  2. The tangent-line error is R1=12f″(c)(x−a)2R_1=\tfrac12 f''(c)(x-a)^2. Predict the sign condition on f″f'' that makes R1≥0R_1\ge0 always — i.e., makes ff stay above all its tangents. (That’s convexity, Chapter 8.)
  3. Newton’s method (Chapter 9) replaces ff by its tangent line and solves. Using the T1T_1 remainder, predict why the error after a Newton step is proportional to the square of the error before.
  4. In problem 17 you found the series of the Gaussian e−x2e^{-x^2}. Its cousin e−x2/2e^{-x^2/2} is the bell curve of probability (Book 4). From the series, predict its symmetry and its single maximum — and notice you now understand the most important curve in statistics as nothing but a reshuffled exponential.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. A function is — almost — nothing more than its derivatives at a single point, reassembled. What is doing the work in that 'almost'?
  2. The remainder term is described as honest accounting of the error. Why does an approximation become far more useful once you know how wrong it is?
  3. The Taylor series of e to the x falls out as inevitable precisely because e to the x is its own derivative. Trace that: why does the self-slope property force those particular coefficients?

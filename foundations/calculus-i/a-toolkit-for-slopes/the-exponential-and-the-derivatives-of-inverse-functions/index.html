The exponential, and the derivatives of inverse functions

The exponential cameo. One function deserves a special mention here and a full chapter (Chapter 5) of its own: exe^x, the function that is its own derivative,

ddxex=ex.\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x.

(From the definition, the difference quotient is ex⋅eh−1he^x\cdot\tfrac{e^h - 1}{h}, and the fact that eh−1h→1\tfrac{e^h-1}{h} \to 1 is precisely what singles out the number ee — the loan you flagged in Chapter 3’s Looking-ahead, repaid in Chapter 5.) Combined with the chain rule, this differentiates every exponential you’ll meet: ddxekx=kekx\tfrac{d}{dx}e^{kx} = k\,e^{kx}, ddxex2=2xex2\tfrac{d}{dx}e^{x^2} = 2x\,e^{x^2}.

Inverse functions. The last tool in the kit, and the one Chapter 5 needs to differentiate the logarithm. Suppose gg is the inverse of ff — meaning f(g(x))=xf(g(x)) = x for all xx. Differentiate both sides with the chain rule: the left side gives f′(g(x))⋅g′(x)f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x), the right side gives 11. Solve:

Inverse-Function Rule. If g=f−1g = f^{-1}, then g′(x)=1f′(g(x)).\displaystyle g'(x) = \frac{1}{f'\!\big(g(x)\big)}.

The picture is clean: an inverse reflects a graph across the line y=xy = x, which swaps “rise” and “run” — so the slope of the inverse at a point is the reciprocal of the slope of the original at the matching point. And it passes an honesty check against Chapter 3: x\sqrt{x} is the inverse of f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2 (for x≥0x \ge 0), and since f′(x)=2xf'(x) = 2x, the rule gives ddxx=1f′(x)=12x\tfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \tfrac{1}{f'(\sqrt{x})} = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} — the same answer you rationalized by hand, now obtained without a single difference quotient. This is exactly the machinery Chapter 5 will turn on ln⁡x\ln x (the inverse of exe^x) to discover that its derivative is 1x\tfrac1x.

With these, you can differentiate essentially every function in this book — no difference quotients required. The art is recognizing which rule (or combination) a given function calls for.

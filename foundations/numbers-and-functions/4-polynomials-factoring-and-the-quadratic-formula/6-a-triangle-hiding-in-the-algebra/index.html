4.6 A triangle hiding in the algebra

Before we crack the quadratic, here’s something delightful — no work required, just notice it. What if we take higher powers of a sum? Yang Hui’s triangle. Each entry is the sum of the two above it (here 3 + 3 = 6), and row n gives the coefficients of (a + b)n. Extend it forever with no multiplying — the next row is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. This is Yang Hui’s triangle — named for the Chinese mathematician who wrote it down in 1261, though it was known to Jia Xian earlier and appears in Persian and Indian mathematics too. (In many Western books it’s called Pascal’s triangle, after a European who studied it four centuries later.) When you reach the probability book, these exact numbers will walk back through the door — because the number of ways to flip a coin and get a certain number of heads is a number in this triangle. For now, just enjoy the pattern.

4.7 When factoring won't cooperate: build your own formula

Factoring by hand works beautifully when the numbers are friendly. But plenty of quadratics have roots that aren’t tidy, and then the guess-two-numbers game just fails. We need something that works every time — and we’re going to build it, so you never have to take it on faith.

The one idea. Square roots undo squares. If I can force a quadratic into the shape (something)2 = a number, then I can square-root both sides and I’m basically done. The bending move is called completing the square, and the name is literal.

Solve x2 + 6x + 5 = 0. Step 1: get the loose number out of the way. Move the +5 across: x2 + 6x = −5. Step 2: complete the square. A perfect square (x + p)2 expands to x2 + 2px + p2. Matching the middle: 2p = 6, so p = 3, and the missing piece is p2 = 9. Add 9 to both sides: x2 + 6x + 9 = −5 + 9 =⇒ (x + 3)2 = 4.

The whole left side collapsed into a single square. Step 3: undo the square — and a square root has two answers, so we write ±: x + 3 = ±2. Step 4: solve. x = −3 ± 2, which means x = −1 or x = −5. There it is — just completing the square, done once and for all, so nobody ever has to do the four steps again.

A formula is frozen thought. You do the hard reasoning once, in general, with letters — and from then on it’s reusable. You can remember the quadratic formula, or, if you forget it, rebuild it from the one idea. The second skill is the one that actually makes you a mathematician.

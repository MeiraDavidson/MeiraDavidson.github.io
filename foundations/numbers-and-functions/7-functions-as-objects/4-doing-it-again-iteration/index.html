7.4 Doing it again: iteration

There’s one special kind of composition so important it deserves its own name. What if you chain a machine to itself — feed its output straight back into its own funnel, and repeat?

x →f(x) →f(f(x)) →f(f(f(x))) →· · · This is iteration, and it is one of the most powerful ideas in all of computation — half of what computers do is iterate. When a machine is defined in terms of itself this way, programmers call it recursion.

Let’s iterate a few machines by hand and watch the long-run behavior, because the long run is the whole point: • Halve it, f(x) = x 2, from 16: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, 1 2, . . . — marches toward 0 and never quite arrives. We call 0 an attractor.

• Multiply by 2, from 1: 1, 2, 4, 8, . . . — runs off to infinity. The behavior flipped just by changing the machine.

• Average x with 2 x, from any positive number: it homes in, startlingly fast, on √ 2. The √ 2-finder converging. Iterating f(x) = 1 2 (x + 2 x ) lands on √ 2 in just a few steps — the ancestor of Newton’s method and gradient descent.

Iteration is how you solve things you can’t solve directly. You can’t write √ 2 as a fraction — but you can write a simple machine whose repeated application converges to it, as close as you like.

In the calculus book you’ll meet Newton’s method, which iterates a clever machine to hunt down the roots of any equation. In the machine-learning book you’ll meet gradient descent — the algorithm that actually trains every neural network — and it, too, is pure iteration: take a step downhill, look again, repeat a few million times. The training of every AI you’ve ever used is one machine, iterated. Hold that thread tightly.

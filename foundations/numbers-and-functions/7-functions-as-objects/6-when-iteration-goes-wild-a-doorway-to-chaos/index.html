7.6 When iteration goes wild: a doorway to chaos

Now something that genuinely unsettled mathematicians in the 1970s — and it needs nothing more than the arithmetic you already have. Take the simplest interesting feedback machine, the logistic map (the iterated cousin of Chapter 5’s S-curve): f(x) = r x (1 −x).

Think of x as a population (a fraction between 0 and 1). The r x part is growth; the (1 −x) part is the ceiling. One knob, r, controls how fast it tries to grow. We iterate: this year’s population in, next year’s out, repeat. Here’s the unsettling part. Just by turning the single knob r, the long-run behavior changes in sudden, violent jumps: • Small r (r = 2): settles to a single steady value. Calm.

• Turn r up (around 3): the steady value splits in two — now it bounces forever between a high year and a low year.

• Turn it up more: two splits into four, then eight — a cascade. • Past about r = 3.57: no pattern at all. The population jumps around forever, never repeating — and yet there’s no randomness anywhere. The rule is fixed and simple; you could compute every step with a pencil. But the behavior is, for all practical purposes, unpredictable.

This is chaos: a simple, fully deterministic rule producing behavior so sensitive it can’t be predicted in practice. It overturned a comfortable assumption held for centuries — that simple causes make simple effects. (It’s also why weather can’t be forecast far ahead: tiny differences now become huge differences later. Why I’m planting this seed now. Look at the shape of what happened: as you slowly turned one knob, the system held steady, held steady — then, at a sharp threshold, abruptly reorganized into something qualitatively new. That pattern — a smooth change in a setting producing a sudden jump in behavior — is called a phase transition, and once you have the eyes for it you see it everywhere: water that cools degree by degree and then, at one temperature, abruptly freezes; and — this is the live frontier — an artificial intelligence that is trained and trained showing no sign of a skill, and then, rather suddenly, acquiring it. The way large AI models seem to snap into new abilities as they grow is, many suspect, this same phenomenon. Nobody fully understands it yet. It is some of the most important unsolved territory in modern science, and it lives right here, in the doorway you’re standing in — a child iterating r x(1 −x) with a pencil is touching the same mathematics.

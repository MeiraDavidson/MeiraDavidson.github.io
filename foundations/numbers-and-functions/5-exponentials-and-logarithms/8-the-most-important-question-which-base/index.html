5.8 The most important question: which base?

So far we’ve doubled — base 2. But nothing forced that. Every base gives a perfectly good exponential. So here’s a question that cracks open the rest of mathematics: Is there a best base — one that makes the machine behave most simply?

To even ask that we need to say what “simply” means, and the honest answer needs calculus. But I can show you the shape of it. Every exponential curve bx climbs, and at each point it has a steepness. An exponential is special: its steepness is always proportional to its own height. For base 2 the steepness is a bit less than the height; for base 3 a bit more. So somewhere between 2 and 3 there must be a magic base where the steepness is exactly equal to the height. That magic base is an irrational number, a little past 2.7: e = 2.71828 . . .

It is called Euler’s number, and it is one of the two or three most important numbers in all of mathematics, sharing that pedestal with π. The function ex is the purest possible growth machine — the one whose growth rate, at every point, equals itself. Its inverse, loge, gets its own symbol, ln (for natural logarithm). I can’t fully justify e until we have derivatives — but when you meet it again, you’ll already know what it is: the number that was hiding between 2 and 3, waiting for the question “which base is best?”

Things worth thinking about 1. We found 21/2 = √ 2. Using the same logic, what should 21/3 be? (What would you multiply together three times to get 2?) Connect this to the cube roots from Chapter 2.

2. The graph of 2x gets closer and closer to zero on the left but never touches it. Why can 2x never actually equal zero, no matter how negative x gets? Problem 7 — Compounding, and its ceiling.

$1000 sits in an account multiplying by 1.05 each year. (a) Write the exponential function for the balance after x years. (b) Roughly how much after 1 year? After 2? (Notice the second year’s gain is bigger — interest earning interest.) (c) The formula says after 300 years the balance is over $2 billion. Explain why no real deposit has ever done this, and sketch what the balance actually would look like. What is the “ceiling,” and what makes it bite?

Going further ⋆Teach it — explain it to someone Problem 8 — Teach it (the sigmoid decision). Explain to a family member how the curve σ(x) = 1 1+e−x “makes a decision.”

Walk them through three inputs: a big positive number (answer near 1 = “yes”), a big negative one (near 0 = “no”), and zero (exactly 1 2 = “unsure”). Then drop the punchline: this exact curve is how a single artificial neuron decides, and it’s the same shape that makes an epidemic level off. If they see one curve doing both jobs, you’ve taught the chapter’s deepest idea.

Problem 9 — [challenge] Half-life, backwards.

A radioactive sample halves every 10 years: the amount left after t years is A(t) = 100 · (1 2)t/10 grams. (a) How much is left after 30 years — without a calculator? (b) Now the hard direction: after how many years is only 25 grams left? Set A(t) = 25 and solve, thinking in “how many halvings?” (c) Write the exact answer for “when is only 10 grams left” using a logarithm. (This is exactly how carbon dating works.)

Problem 10 — [challenge] The log scale’s superpower.

An earthquake’s energy grows by a factor of about 32 for each whole step up the Richter scale. (a) How many times more energy does a magnitude-7 quake release than a magnitude-5? (b) Explain, using log(xy) = log x + log y, why turning “multiply energy by 32” into “add 1 to the magnitude” is what makes the scale usable by humans. (c) Why would a linear energy scale — plain joules — be hopeless for comparing earthquakes you read about in the news? Problem 11 — [challenge] Stacked exponents.

Which is bigger, 2(32) or (23)2? Compute both. (The lesson: exponent towers are read top-down, and (am)n = amn is not the same as a(mn) — a trap worth feeling once with your own hands.)

The Light That Stays

4. Once You See It, You Can’t Unsee It

So what happens if you keep at it?

Meditation doesn’t make life’s hard things disappear; nothing can do that. What it does is stranger, and better.

At some point, if you keep practicing, you get a peek. Not at a quiet, empty stillness — at something more alive than that. The part of you that’s just aware, underneath all the noise, turns out to carry a kind of wakeful brightness and a quiet, causeless gladness. Not gladness about anything. Just gladness, the kind that doesn’t need a reason. People call this catching a glimpse of “the nature of your mind.” It’s hard to describe — like trying to explain the color blue to someone who’s never seen it. But here’s what I can promise: once you’ve felt it, you can’t unfeel it. You’ll know, forever, that it’s there — even on the days the clouds cover it completely.

And it changes you. Quietly, from the inside.

You start to feel that every living thing — every person, every animal, every annoying classmate, every stranger — has the same nature of mind that you do. They’re not you, of course. They have their own lives, their own hopes, their own hurts; they want to be happy as much as you do. But the awake, knowing quality underneath all of that — the same watcher we’ve been finding inside you all through this book — is also inside them. Same nature, different lives. And once you really feel that, cruelty stops making sense. How could you be cruel to a being who, underneath everything, is reaching for the same things you’re reaching for, made of the same wakeful stuff? You find yourself caring about others almost without trying.

You also get tougher in a soft way. When something goes wrong, you don’t get knocked over as easily, because you’re standing on something that bad days can’t reach. And you start to notice small joys you used to walk right past — a flower at the edge of the sidewalk, a dog tilting its head when you say its name, the smell of something good cooking, the warmth of sun on your shoulders, a stranger’s small kindness on a bus, a tree on your street suddenly looking beautiful, a piece of food you actually taste instead of just swallow, a friend laughing, a good breath. They were always there. They’re everywhere, honestly — and a practice is what slowly trains your eyes to see them again.

I say slowly on purpose, because I want to be honest with you. If life has been heavy for a while, you may already have noticed that the things people say should bring joy don’t seem to anymore. The food doesn’t taste like much. The flowers don’t really land. The thing that used to make you laugh still makes you smile, but only for a second. That isn’t because the joys aren’t there. It’s because heaviness puts a kind of film over your eyes, and the film takes time to come off.

So try this — gently. Keep your short daily practice going — the breath-watching, the body scan, the one-minute reset, whatever’s become yours — even when it doesn’t feel like it’s doing anything. And turn back toward whatever used to bring you the most joy — but not by sitting around doing nothing. When you’re already heavy, that kind of empty, inert time tends to deepen the weight. Reach instead for things that actually engage you. They can be quiet or loud, fast or slow, as long as they really pull you in: dancing to a song you love, playing with your dog, calling a friend who makes you feel like yourself, rock climbing, a math puzzle you couldn’t put down, a sport you used to play hard, running, swimming, biking, drawing, building something, a book that grips you so hard you forget the time. Whatever used to light you up.

One I want to single out, because almost nothing else on earth shifts a heavy mood as reliably: moving your body, hard enough to get sweaty. Tennis or pickleball with one other person, a long bike ride, swimming until your arms are tired, a walk that turns into a run. The science behind this is solid and the effect is real — exercise is one of the most underrated things you can do for your mind, not just your body. (Team sports work too, of course — but they need a whole group of playmates, and if your usual crowd has gone strange on you, finding one can be its own hill to climb. Better to start with something that needs only you, or just one other person who’s still around for you.) The opposite of heaviness isn’t loud — it’s engaged. A math puzzle is quiet from outside but very alive inside. That counts. What doesn’t help is the doing-nothing, sitting-around kind of time.

Let yourself stay with it a little longer than feels normal. There’s a voice inside heaviness that whispers yeah, but you don’t really feel it anymore — and that voice is part of the film, not the truth. The thing you loved is still there. Stay with it anyway. Slowly the film thins. The flower lands. The dog’s head-tilt is funny again. Hard times don’t get the last word in a life that keeps practicing — and slowly, you find yourself meeting them with a kind of stubborn, gentle optimism.

That — not relaxation, not being good at sitting still — is the whole point of meditation. Everything else is just practice for this.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. He says once you feel that the same awake quality is in everyone, cruelty stops making sense. Do you think understanding can change behaviour on its own, or does it take more?
  2. The chapter describes becoming 'tougher in a soft way'. What would that look like in someone you know? How is it different from just being tough?
  3. There is a voice inside heaviness that whispers you don't really feel it anymore — and he says that voice is part of the film, not the truth. Why is it useful to be told about that voice in advance?

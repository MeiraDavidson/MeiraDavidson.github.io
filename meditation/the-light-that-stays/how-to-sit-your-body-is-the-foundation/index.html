The Light That Stays

5. How to Sit — Your Body Is the Foundation

Before we ask the big question — who is seeing? — we have to do something first. We have to sit.

This is the part most meditation books skip, and skipping it is exactly why so many people try meditation, feel nothing, and quit. Here’s the secret nobody told them: you can’t bully your mind into being calm. But you can arrange your body — and the mind quietly follows.

There’s an old image I love. Your mind is like a rider, and your breath is the horse it rides on. Where the horse goes, the rider goes. If the horse is bucking and galloping, the rider gets bounced all over the place. But if the horse slows to a gentle walk, the rider settles right down. Your breath is that horse — and the way you sit decides how the horse moves. Slump over and squash your lungs, and the breath turns short and jumpy, and so does your mind. Sit tall and open, and the breath grows long and smooth, and the mind grows calm and bright almost by itself.

So we start with the body. Always the body first.

The Seven Points of a Good Seat

Long ago, meditators worked out seven small adjustments that turn your body into a steady, comfortable seat — like a cup that can hold still water without spilling it. You don’t have to be bendy or athletic. You just line up these seven points and let the rest go.

1. Your legs — cross them, and build a wide, steady base. The most famous pose is the lotus, where each foot rests up on the opposite thigh. It makes a beautiful, rock-solid triangle, like a little mountain that can’t tip over. If it’s comfortable for you, wonderful.

But listen closely, because this part matters: never force it. Your body is still growing, and jamming your knees down into a full lotus can injure them for real. There is no prize for a hurt knee. A half-lotus (just one foot up) is great. Plain cross-legged is great. Kneeling with a cushion is great. And sitting in a chair with your feet flat on the floor is completely, totally fine — plenty of lifelong meditators sit in chairs. The pose only exists to keep you steady. It is not the meditation.

One thing that’s actually important enough that I should stop calling it a “trick”: put a cushion under your butt. Sit on the front edge of a firm cushion, a folded blanket, or even a stack of pillows — anything that lifts your hips a couple of inches above your knees. This is the move that lets your spine stay straight without you having to fight for it. With a little practice you’ll find your own right height; most people land somewhere between two and five inches. Without that lift, almost everyone slumps or strains. With it, the posture nearly holds itself.

2. Your hands — let them rest. Either lay them palms-down on your knees, or rest them in your lap, one hand cupped in the other, thumbs barely touching to make a soft oval. Then forget about them. Let them be heavy.

3. Your spine — straight, like a stack of coins. This is the master key. If you remember only one point on this whole list, remember this one. Picture your backbone as a tall stack of coins, each balanced right on the one below. The whole spine should feel long, as if a soft string were attached to the very top of your head, gently lifting it upward — the crown of your head floating at the highest point of you, like a balloon held up by a thread from above. And then — relax. Really, relax. Straight but not stiff. Tall but not tense. Everything soft except that one quiet thread of upward lift. Here’s the practical move that actually gets your crown to that high, balanced spot: the small chin tuck in point 5 below. That tiny tuck does almost all the work. Get those two right — long spine, chin tucked, crown lifting on its own — and the rest more or less take care of themselves: the shoulders drop, the head balances, the breath travels all the way down. The whole posture sits itself.

4. Your shoulders — open, like a bird’s wings. Roll them gently back and let them drop. The old teachers said to open the chest “like a vulture spreading its wings.” It just means: don’t hunch. Let the space around your heart be open.

5. Your chin — tucked just a little. Small but secretly important — this is the move that makes point 3 actually work. Drop your chin a tiny bit, like a small hook, so the back of your neck grows long. Not chin-on-chest — just the slightest tuck. With it, the head balances right on top of the spine and the crown floats up to the highest point of you all on its own. Without it, the head tends to poke forward and the whole posture droops with it. So small, so important: tuck the chin a little, and your spine does the rest.

6. Your eyes — soft, and (if you like) a little open. Here’s a surprise: you don’t have to shut your eyes. Many traditions — including the one closest to my own heart — keep the eyes slightly open, gaze soft and lowered, resting on a spot on the floor a few feet ahead without really looking at anything. Open eyes keep you from drifting off to sleep. If open feels too busy while you’re learning, close them — that’s fine too. Soft, either way.

7. Your mouth — relaxed, tongue resting up. Let your lips and teeth come slightly apart, soft, almost a hint of a smile. Rest the tip of your tongue gently on the ridge just behind your upper front teeth. Then breathe through your nose, naturally. Don’t do anything to the breath yet. Just let it come and go.

Seven points. Run through them once at the start, top to bottom — then leave your body alone. You’re not a soldier standing at attention. You’re a mountain that has found its balance and can sit, easy and awake, for a long, long time.

One thing to underline before we move on: don’t stress your body trying to do all seven of these. The two that matter most are the spine and the chin tuck. Get those right, and the others mostly fall into place on their own. Everything past that — relax. If you find yourself gripping or straining to hold the posture, you’ve already lost the thread. The seat is not one more thing to be good at; it is just the shape that lets you let go.

The Body Scan — Settling In, Breath by Breath

Now that you’re sitting well, here is the practice I’d put in your hands first if I could give you only one. It’s called a body scan, and it trains your body and your breath to settle down together. Read it through once, then try it. Ten minutes is plenty. Even three is real.

Arrive. In your seven points, take three slow, deliberate breaths: in through the nose, then a long, slow breath out through the mouth, like you’re gently fogging a mirror. With each out-breath, let your whole body grow a little heavier, a little softer. After the third one, let the breath go back to normal, in and out through the nose. You don’t steer it now. You just watch it, the way you’d watch waves roll in and slide back out.

Now travel slowly down through your body, riding the breath.

  • Bring your attention to the very top of your head. Breathe in and shine your attention there, like a soft flashlight. Breathe out, and let the top of your head soften.
  • Slide the light down to your face. Forehead — let it smooth. Eyes — let them grow heavy. Jaw — this one’s sneaky; we clench it without knowing. Breathe out and let your jaw come loose.
  • Down to your neck and shoulders. Almost everyone carries their whole day right here. Breathe in, notice them — then on the out-breath, let them drop, lower and looser than you thought they could go.
  • Down your arms — upper arms, elbows, forearms, all the way to your hands. Notice your hands. Maybe warm, maybe tingly, maybe heavy, maybe nothing much. Whatever’s there is fine. You’re not making a feeling happen. You’re just noticing what’s already there.
  • Into your chest. Feel it rise and fall on its own. You don’t lift it. It breathes you.
  • Into your belly. Let it go soft, moving with the breath like a balloon gently filling and emptying.
  • Down your back, one coin of the spine at a time. Strong and easy.
  • Into your hips, sitting heavy. Feel the ground holding you up. You don’t have to hold yourself — the earth’s got you.
  • Down your legs — thighs, knees, shins — to your feet. Feel your feet. Feel exactly where they touch the floor.

Here’s the part that turns this from a nap into meditation. Resting there, feeling your whole body at once, gently ask: Who is feeling all of this? The warmth in the hands, the rise of the chest, the weight of sitting — someone is aware of every bit of it. That someone is you — not the sensations, but the one who knows them. Don’t strain for an answer. Just let the question turn your attention back toward the quiet one who’s been watching all along. (That’s the big question of the next chapter — and look, you’re already standing at its door.)

Rest. For a last little while, stop traveling. Just sit, breathing, whole. Body settled, breath slow — the rider quiet now, on a horse that’s slowed to a gentle walk. Nothing to do, nowhere to be. Stay as long as it stays easy. Then open your eyes the rest of the way and carry a thread of that stillness out into your day.

Ending with a Small Dedication

There’s one more piece I love to add at the very end of any formal sit, just before you open your eyes. It takes a few seconds, and it quietly changes the whole practice.

Before you get up, take one more slow breath, and send out a wish — the way you’d wish on a falling star. The wish can be anything, as long as it’s kind. You can wish for someone you love (may my mom be happy). You can wish for yourself (may I be gentle with myself today). You can wish for people you don’t even know (may anyone having a hard day feel held). You can wish for the whole world (may it be a little gentler tomorrow). You can wish for a cause you care about (may more kids have books to read, may fewer animals be hurt).

The wish doesn’t do anything magical out in the world. What it does is point your heart outward for a moment, before you stand up. The meditation built up something quiet and good inside you; the wish is how you give a little of it away. A practice that ends in a wish, over the years, builds a person who’s bigger of heart, not smaller.

One breath, one wish, one quiet may this be so, and you’re done.

The Three-Breath Version (for busy days)

No ten minutes today? Sit up — spine like coins. Take three slow breaths, and with each one, drop your shoulders, soften your jaw, and feel your feet on the floor. That’s a real meditation. Tiny is still real.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. 'You can't bully your mind into being calm. But you can arrange your body — and the mind quietly follows.' Test it: sit up tall, drop your shoulders, and breathe out slowly three times. Did anything shift?
  2. The rider and the horse: where the breath goes, the mind goes. Can you think of another pair in your life where fixing the simpler thing fixes the harder one?
  3. He says never force the lotus position, because your body is still growing. Why is 'do it properly' sometimes the wrong advice?

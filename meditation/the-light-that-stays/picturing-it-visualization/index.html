The Light That Stays

9. Picturing It — Visualization

Your imagination is powerful. Meditation can use it on purpose. Here are a few pictures to try while you sit with your eyes closed.

The Sky and the Sun

Imagine your mind is a huge, wide-open sky. Your thoughts and feelings are clouds and storms drifting across it — some dark, some fluffy and bright. You don’t chase them or push them away. You just watch them pass. You’re not the weather. You’re the sky.

But don’t stop at empty sky. Remember Chapter 6: high above all the clouds, the sun is always shining — warm, bright, never once switched off. The storms can hide it, but they can’t put it out. That sun stands for the joy and wakefulness that are your real nature — a picture to lean on while you practice, not a real light to go find. On a hard day, picture the clouds rolling in front of it, and then remember: the sun didn’t go anywhere. It’s still up there, warming the top of every cloud. This one picture can carry you through a whole lifetime of hard days.

Your Favorite Place in Nature

Now picture, somewhere in the world, a place in nature where you feel completely at peace. Maybe it’s a beach you’ve been to once. A meadow. A trail in the woods behind your house. A place from a book or a movie. Real or imagined — it doesn’t matter, as long as it feels like yours.

For me, my place is the top of a tropical mountain at night. The sky above me is full of stars — more than I usually see, every one of them clear and bright. Far below, the dark ocean stretches out to the horizon, boundless. The air is soft and warm. Nothing in the world needs me. I’m just there, sitting, held by the dark sky and the wide world.

Find your version of that. Build it in your mind. Notice the sky, the ground, the air on your skin, the sounds in the distance. Sit there in your meditation as if you were sitting there in person. Let the place hold you. The body in your room and the body in that place are doing the same thing — being still, being held. That’s the whole point.

Sitting on a Lotus

Here’s one more picture for your sit. Imagine, underneath you as you meditate, a giant lotus flower has opened. Its broad, soft pink petals are spread wide like a great cushion, and you’re sitting right in the middle, held up by the bloom.

Here’s why the lotus is the perfect image. The lotus grows in mud. Its roots are buried deep in the muck at the bottom of a pond — dark, slimy, ordinary. But somehow the stem rises up through the murky water, breaks the surface, and opens into a clean, perfect flower in the sun. The mud doesn’t ruin it. In fact, the lotus can’t bloom without the mud. The flower and the muck are part of the same plant.

That’s a picture of you. Your life has its mud — the hard days, the rejections, the messes you didn’t ask for and didn’t choose. The lotus doesn’t pretend the mud isn’t there. It just keeps growing up through it, and what blooms at the top is the open, awake, kind person you’re already becoming. Sit on the lotus. Let the mud be the mud. Bloom anyway.

And here’s a beautiful thing you can do once you’re seated on your lotus. Imagine, just above the crown of your head, a soft rainbow light. As you breathe in, that light begins to pour gently in through the top of your head — inside you, not over you — and flows slowly down through the middle of your body, all the way to your feet: through your face, your neck, your chest, your belly, your hips, your legs.

Picture the light carrying away whatever heaviness you’ve been holding: tiredness, worry, the ache of a hard day, sadness that’s been sitting on your chest, an argument you keep replaying in your head. As it flows down through you, the light dissolves it all and carries it down — out the bottom, through the lotus, into the mud where the roots are. Even your hardest stuff becomes nourishment for the bloom. By the time the light has reached your feet, you feel cleaner, lighter, refreshed.

You don’t have to believe in any of this for it to work. You’re just using your imagination to help your body let go and your heart open.

The Warm Light

Imagine a soft, warm light glowing in the center of your chest. With each breath, it grows a little brighter and spreads a little wider — out through your whole body, then out to your family, your friends, even people you find difficult, until it fills the whole room and beyond. This is an old way of growing kindness on purpose. (It connects to what we said in Chapter 4: that the same light is in everyone.)

Mix Them Together

These pictures aren’t four separate exercises — they’re different doors into the same room, and you can walk through several at once. Picture yourself sitting in your favorite place in nature — your beach, your meadow, the top of your mountain. Now imagine a great lotus has opened up underneath you, holding you up off the ground. And from just above the crown of your head, the rainbow light begins to pour down through you, carrying away whatever heaviness you brought to the cushion. That’s the fullest version: the place holds you, the lotus holds you up, the light moves through. You don’t have to do all of it every time — some days one door is enough. Whatever feels right.

The pictures themselves don’t matter. The feeling matters: peace, serenity, a body that’s let go of something it had been carrying, a small refreshed glow when you open your eyes. If you sit for ten minutes and come back with a little of that — by whatever door — the visualization worked.

Letting the Picture Go

After a while, let the picture fade and just rest in the quiet that’s left. The pictures are training wheels. The real ride is the stillness underneath.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The sky-and-sun picture: you are not the weather, you are the sky — and above the clouds the sun was never switched off. Why is that a more useful image on a hard day than simply 'stay calm'?
  2. He describes his own place — a tropical mountain at night, stars, the dark ocean far below. Where is yours? It can be real or invented. Describe it in enough detail that someone else could picture it.
  3. 'The pictures are training wheels. The real ride is the stillness underneath.' When is it right to let go of a tool that is working?

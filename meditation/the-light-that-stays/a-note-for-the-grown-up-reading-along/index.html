The Light That Stays

A Note for the Grown-Up Reading Along

This is a book for young people — from about twelve onward, and just as readable for teenagers and grown-ups — about a simple, ancient idea: that there is a warm, awake, quietly joyful light inside each of us, and that we can learn to find it on purpose.

A few honest words before you hand it over.

Meditation is not a magic trick and it is not medicine. It helps a great many people feel steadier, kinder, and more able to enjoy small things — and there’s good science behind that. But it is not a cure for everything. If a child in your life is carrying a sadness that won’t lift, or thoughts that frighten them, the bravest and smartest thing is not to meditate harder — it’s to tell a trusted grown-up: a parent, a teacher, a doctor, a counselor. Some feelings need a caring person, not just a cushion. You’ll find that message gently repeated in the book itself, because I want every reader to hear it.

The practices and ideas in this book come from the Buddhist contemplative tradition — particularly the Zen and Tibetan Nyingma schools where I trained. You’ll see that openly: a sutra or two, a mantra, a few names from the tradition. They’re offered here as universal tools, though; you don’t have to be Buddhist, or anything else, for any of them to help. But you deserve to know where they come from.

Everything here is offered as a friend, not an authority. Use what helps. Leave the rest.

Questions to talk about

No answer key. These are for arguing over, with someone who read the same chapter.

  1. The author says plainly that meditation is not a cure for everything, and that some feelings need a caring person rather than a cushion. Why put that warning at the very front instead of the back?
  2. He names exactly where the practices come from — Zen and Tibetan Nyingma — while offering them as tools anyone can use. Why does he think you deserve to know the source even if you never take up the tradition?
  3. 'Everything here is offered as a friend, not an authority.' What changes about how you read a book when the author says that first?
